## What it is

The statewide *Alabama Mental Health Systems Report* describes a mental health system by the jobs it has to do. Its list of jobs follows the World Health Organization's six building blocks, a description of what any health system needs: service delivery, a health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership and governance [397]. To those six it adds two domains of its own, on equity and on community engagement [397]. Four further sections cover systems outside the Department of Mental Health, which are children, justice, social determinants and payers [397].

The statewide report called itself a baseline for "a planned North Alabama regional edition built on the same template" [397]. This report is that edition. It keeps the eight domains and the four sections and narrows the view to two counties.

In this region one organization holds several roles at once. WellStone is the community mental health center for both counties [2]. It runs the crisis center that the Department assigns to fifteen counties [3], and one of Alabama's four in-state 988 call centers is at WellStone [4]. A report organized around that one provider would leave out much of what the domains bring in, such as a city council that voted to fund police co-responders [63] or a Cullman hospital whose needs assessment ranks mental health first [83].

Label the frame around all twelve boxes *the core: Madison and Cullman counties, 527,525 residents* [6]. Outside the frame, draw a band labeled *Section 13, the ring: thirteen counties, four other centers, 792,981 residents* [2] [6]. One arrow runs from the band to Domain 3 and is labeled *crisis center only*, because the fifteen-county list applies to WellStone Emergency Services and the pediatric unit [3].

## How it works in north Alabama

Following one service through the report shows the design. In Domain 2 the pediatric crisis unit is a $9 million building, credited by WellStone to the Legislature, the Department and campaign donors without the share of each [91]. In Domain 3 it is a service that opened in late September 2024 with 24 beds, where the Department counts 520 people served in FY2025 [43] [64]. Domain 4 treats it as evidence of a staffing shortage [56]. Domain 6 sets the Department's 520 beside WellStone's own figure of 400 individuals in the unit's first year [64] [69]. Section 9 places it among children's services as a crisis unit that can hold a patient for up to 14 days [167].

Putting each question to two counties also turns every domain into a comparison. County Health Rankings counts 17.1% of Madison County's population as rural and 75.6% of Cullman's [20].

## How this report uses it

<!-- framing -->
The domains work as an index of questions: who holds a decision is a matter for Domain 1, and whether a number exists is one for Domain 6. Each domain and section closes with "Gaps and opportunities". Those bullets are the report's own reading of the evidence, marked as analysis, and should be quoted that way.

The dependency map in Part IV uses the same twelve parts as the statewide map. One of its links runs from payers to services: the state requires a certified clinic to serve anyone who seeks care and pays the clinic's daily rate only for people enrolled in Medicaid [374] [57].

Two cautions apply to the frame at this scale. The figures in Parts II and III mostly describe the core, with standing exceptions: counts for the crisis center cover all fifteen counties of its catchment [64], and the homelessness counts in Section 11 cover the three counties of the Huntsville continuum [311]. And a tool built for comparing counties can miss what is happening inside one. The federal shortage measure gives Madison County no area designation, which makes it the only Alabama county without one [52], and that is the county where, a month after it opened, the pediatric unit could staff six of its 24 beds [56].
