## What it is

Medicaid is the public health insurance program for low-income people, run by each state within federal rules and paid for jointly by the federal government and the state [397]. The Affordable Care Act let states extend Medicaid to adults with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty level, and Alabama is one of ten states that have not done so [397]. Its income limit for parents is about 18 percent of the poverty level, and adults without children or a disability are not eligible at any income [397].

The other route to coverage for people without a job-based plan is the health insurance marketplace, where individuals buy private plans with tax credits that lower the premium [397]. Those subsidies start at the poverty line [397]. An adult whose income is above the state's Medicaid limit and below the poverty line therefore falls between the two programs, and that space is called the coverage gap [397].

About half of the people Alabama Medicaid enrolls are children [397]. The agency says more than 50 percent of the state's children are covered, and about 25 percent of all residents at some point in a year [58]. Children in families with somewhat higher incomes are covered by a separate children's insurance program [361].

## How it works in north Alabama

**Who is covered.** Madison County relies more on employer coverage than Cullman, where public programs cover a larger share: Census survey estimates for 2024 put 53.2% of Madison residents on employer plans against 41.9% in Cullman, and Medicaid at 9.77% in Madison and 15.9% in Cullman [354] [355]. The uninsured share on those all-ages estimates was 7.72% in Madison and 10.9% in Cullman [354] [355]. Among people under 65 it was 8.3% and 11.9% [356] [357].

In April 2026 the Medicaid agency counted 54,803 eligible people in Madison County and 15,922 in Cullman, both down from a year earlier [358]. It also reports that 18% of Madison's population was eligible at some point in FY2024, one of the five lowest shares in the state [58]. That slide prints only the highest and lowest counties, so Cullman's share is not given [58]. The children's insurance program covered 12,741 children in Madison and 4,757 in Cullman in July 2026 [361].

No local estimate of the coverage gap could be found: the two statewide studies read give no county figures [366].

**What it means for the clinic.** Under Medicaid's rule a provider of rehabilitative mental health services must be certified by the Department of Mental Health as a community mental health center, which in these two counties means WellStone [367]. Since July 2024 WellStone has also been a Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic [42]. A clinic of that kind must serve anyone who seeks help, regardless of place of residence, ability to pay or age [374]. For each day on which a Medicaid enrollee receives a qualifying service, it is paid a single daily rate [371]. The state's manual says the rate "will not be paid for services provided to populations outside of those enrolled in Medicaid" [57]. WellStone publishes its funding sources as a list of categories with no payer mix, so how many of its clients are outside Medicaid could not be established [1].

**Uncompensated care.** WellStone states that no one is turned away for inability to pay and reports nearly $3.4 million in uncompensated care for fiscal year 2025 [48]. Its plan names funding for medications for adult clients "with no third-party reimbursement" as one of three unmet needs [1]. Hospitals carry such costs on a larger scale: the Huntsville Hospital system's audit puts uncompensated care at about $144.4 million for the year ended June 2025, down from $171.4 million, a fall it attributes mainly to higher Medicaid access payments [125].

**Hospital stays.** Medicaid's psychiatric facility rule covers inpatient psychiatric care for recipients under 21 [369]. For enrollees aged 21 to 64, Alabama's federal waiver pays for stays in institutions for mental disease only in Baldwin and Mobile counties, and no north Alabama facility is covered [370].

**Marketplace and commercial coverage.** After the enhanced marketplace tax credits expired at the end of 2025, about 95,000 Alabamians, some 23% of the state's enrollees, left the marketplace [363]. A hospital president in the Huntsville system had estimated beforehand that about 19,500 of those expected to lose coverage lived in the system's north Alabama territory [364]. The federal county-level file for 2026 could not be opened [365]. In the commercial market, Blue Cross and Blue Shield of Alabama held 82% of the Huntsville metropolitan area at the start of 2024 [376].

Section 12 sets out the coverage picture and the clinic payment. The seventh finding in Part I states the tension in two sentences, and the dependency map makes the link from payers to services the first of the three it says carry most weight. Domain 2 reports the uncompensated care and Domain 5 the medication need.

Three Medicaid figures for Madison County measure different things, and the reader should keep them apart. The 9.77% is a survey estimate of people covered in 2024 [354]. The 54,803 is the agency's count of eligibles in one month [358]. The 18% counts anyone eligible at some point during the fiscal year, which is higher than a single month's count [58]. That 18% has nothing to do with the 18 percent of the poverty level that is Alabama's income limit for parents [397]. The children's program should not be added to the agency's county count, because neither table says whether the two overlap [361].

What the report cannot show is the size of the gap locally. No county estimate of the coverage gap was found [366]. The 2026 marketplace losses are known for the state, and the 19,500 is a hospital executive's estimate made before the subsidies expired [363] [364]. The Huntsville system's audit, as read, contains no figure for behavioral health revenue, cost or volume [125].
