## What it is

A shortage of mental health workers is measured in several ways, by different bodies and for different purposes, and the measures do not always agree [397]. This report uses three: a federal designation, a ratio of residents to providers, and what local employers say they cannot staff.

The first is the Health Professional Shortage Area. The federal health workforce agency designates shortage areas for primary, dental and mental health care, and a designation can cover a geographic area, a population within an area, or a facility [397]. For mental health the designation counts psychiatrists only [146]. The threshold is a population of at least 30,000 for each psychiatrist, or 20,000 where needs are unusually high [147]. A designation for a low-income population is narrower: it shows a shortage of providers serving Medicaid and sliding-fee patients, measured against the low-income population [146]. The designation is also a gateway, because federal loan repayment for clinicians is tied to the shortage score of the site where they work [163].

The second measure is a ratio of residents to mental health providers of all kinds [397]. County Health Rankings counts psychiatrists, psychologists, licensed clinical social workers, counselors, marriage and family therapists, providers who treat alcohol and other drug abuse, and advanced practice nurses specializing in mental health [150]. It takes them from the national registry of provider identifiers [150]. The publisher's own caveat is that some providers on the list "may no longer be practicing or accepting new patients," which can overstate supply [150]. A ratio of this kind counts licensed people, not hours worked, and says nothing about whether they take Medicaid [397].

The third measure comes from inside the system: the posts and beds an employer cannot fill [397]. In this region it reaches the public through a strategic plan, press reports and a hospital's needs assessment [1] [56] [83].

## How it works in north Alabama

Cullman County is a mental health shortage area for its low-income population, with a score of 16 [52]. Madison County has no area designation and is the only county in Alabama without one [52]. That absence is a statement about psychiatrists, since no other profession is counted [146]. Sites in Madison County could still hold a facility designation of their own, and whether WellStone's sites do was not established [163] [52]. All thirteen ring counties carry a designation, three of them (Cherokee, DeKalb and Etowah) flagged as proposed for withdrawal [52].

For the ratio there are two published numbers for each county and a third for Cullman. County Health Rankings' March 2026 figures give 474 residents per mental health provider in Madison and 568 in Cullman, against 639 for Alabama [52]. The Kids Count county profiles print 500 and 609 for 2024 [148] [149]. Cullman Regional's needs assessment reports 132.0 providers per 100,000 residents in Cullman County for 2024, against 140.9 for the state and 312.5 for the country [83].

Part of the difference lies in the counting rules. The hospital's assessment draws on the same federal registry as County Health Rankings and names a shorter list of professions: psychiatrists, psychologists, clinical social workers and counselors [83] [150]. The Kids Count profiles print no source or definition for their row [149]. County Health Rankings also refreshes its registry data between releases, so two ratios for the same county can describe different years [150]. On the Rankings measure both counties look better than Alabama; on the hospital's measure Cullman is below the state and far below the nation [52] [83]. Across the ring the Rankings ratio runs from 339 residents per provider in Marshall to 4,774 in Winston [20].

The plainest local evidence is a building. WellStone's pediatric crisis unit opened in late September 2024 with 24 beds [43]. On 23 October it could treat six children at a time because of a staffing shortage [56]. No later report read for this edition says when, or whether, the unit reached full staffing [56]. A year later WellStone's strategic plan named "recruiting and retaining a qualified nursing workforce" among its three greatest unmet needs, with no vacancy or turnover figure [1]. The Huntsville Hospital system said in 2023 that recruiting and retaining employees, "especially nurses," was its top priority [151]. A participant in Cullman Regional's assessment wrote that there are "never enough psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses" [83].

Training is the longer route. Doctors tend to settle near where they train, which is why residency places matter to the supply of psychiatrists [397]. The University of Alabama at Birmingham runs a four-year psychiatry residency at its Huntsville campus, approved for up to six residents a year, with Huntsville Hospital as its clinical site [152] [153]. Its page listed six first-year and six second-year residents in October 2026 [154]. The American Medical Association's residency database lists a second four-year program in Huntsville under WellStone's name, with six first-year positions [156]. That entry shows 24 current residents, a figure that may describe approved positions, and whether the program has residents in training could not be established [156].

## How this report uses it

Domain 4 sets the three measures side by side and then leans on the third, because designations and national ratios work poorly at county scale. Domain 7 uses the designation and the ratio to compare the two counties, and Appendix A prints both for each of the fifteen counties [52] [20].

What the report cannot give is a number for the shortage itself. No vacancy or turnover figure for behavioral health nursing was found for WellStone or for the hospital system [1] [151]. The state health department publishes its mental health shortage designations only as a map, dated May 2024, which could not be read reliably for the two counties; the report takes its designations from the federal file [146] [52].

When a workforce figure appears, check which measure it is, who is counted and which year it describes. The two forms of the ratio also run in opposite directions: 474 residents per provider is better than 568, while 132.0 providers per 100,000 is worse than 140.9 [52] [83]. And a ratio built from a registry describes people who hold an identifier, some of whom may no longer be practicing [150].
