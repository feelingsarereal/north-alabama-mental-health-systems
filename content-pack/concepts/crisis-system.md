## What it is

Federal guidance describes a crisis system as three essential elements: someone to contact, someone to respond, and a safe place for help [397]. The first is a phone line, and since July 2022 the national number for it has been 988 [397]. The second is a mobile crisis team, clinicians who travel to a person in crisis at home or elsewhere in the community [397]. The third is a crisis center, a walk-in place open at all hours where a person can be assessed and stabilized instead of going to an emergency room or a jail [397].

The parts depend on each other. A call line with nowhere to send people, or a center nobody is brought to, does little [397]. A Huntsville business paper, writing in 2022 while the local center was being built, named the two places where a person in crisis might otherwise end up: "The local hospital emergency room or jail" [33].

Steering a person away from those two places and into care is called diversion [397]. It is hard to count, because counting a diversion requires a judgment about what would have happened to the person otherwise [397].

## How it works in north Alabama

**Someone to contact.** One of Alabama's four in-state 988 call centers is at WellStone [4]. The Department's FY2025 table gives it 6,627 of the 35,118 recorded for the four centers, without saying whether the unit is calls or people [64]. The Department's board was told in November 2025 that the state answer rate had been above 90% since July, and no rate is published for Huntsville alone [115]. State opioid settlement money has gone to the line as well: the Department gave WellStone $660,000 of a $3,000,000 appropriation to expand staffing and hours at the north Alabama call center [45]. Crisis Services of North Alabama runs a separate 24-hour HELPline [116].

**Someone to respond.** Mobile crisis began in Cullman, where a state-funded adult team was announced as a pilot in June 2020 [31]. A team member said in September 2022 that it had intervened in more than 150 crises in the county that summer [117]. Huntsville's adult team came later, with a federal grant awarded in 2022 [118] [119]. WellStone's child and adolescent teams cover both counties and served 498 young people in FY2025 [120] [64]. In Huntsville the City Council voted on 13 May 2021 to support a plan for two master's-level clinicians to work alongside crisis-trained police officers, and two were riding with officers by February 2022 [34] [63].

**A safe place for help.** The state named WellStone one of its first three crisis center awardees on 28 October 2020 [32]. A temporary center opened in May 2021 [33]. The permanent building, WellStone Emergency Services, had its ribbon cut on 19 September 2022 and was built for up to 15 temporary observation beds and up to 24 extended observation beds, starting with 16 [38] [88]. At the temporary site the center had averaged 45 to 50 people a month [88]. By September 2024 the adult unit was admitting 175 a month, and WellStone's plan of November 2025 says "over 150" [121] [1]. The Department counts 1,879 people served there in FY2025, a figure that covers all fifteen counties of the catchment [64].

The pediatric unit opened next door in late September 2024 with 24 beds for ages 12 to 18 [43]. A month later a staffing shortage meant it could treat six patients at a time [56]. The Department counts 520 served in FY2025 [64]. WellStone, for its part, reported 400 individuals served in the unit's first year and said it had begun taking children as young as eight [69].

Cullman County has the first two parts and not the third. WellStone said in 2024 that Cullman callers to 988 reach the Huntsville center, and its chief operating officer told a town hall in October 2024 that it has crisis response teams in Cullman and no local crisis facility [41] [169].

## How this report uses it

<!-- framing -->
Domain 3 takes the three parts in order. Domain 2 follows the money and Domain 5 the buildings and the transport to them. Section 10 meets the same services again as Intercept 0, the point before any arrest, and Section 13 asks how much the thirteen ring counties use a center that stands in Huntsville.

What the report can show is that the parts exist and are used. What it cannot show is diversion. WellStone's chief executive said in 2022 that the main purpose of the crisis beds "is to divert individuals from our jails and our emergency departments" [88]. The state evaluation office's 2025 report gives no admissions, lengths of stay, law enforcement drop-offs or diversion figures for Huntsville [62]. It found the centers' monthly reports inconsistent and noted that one center, not named in the passages read, has always reported zero for jail avoidance [62]. The police co-responder program is in the same position: the City's 2022 account gives no call or outcome counts [63].

The savings claimed locally come without their workings. The chief executive said in March 2024 that the jail diversion program was saving about $300,000 of taxpayer money a year "the last time we measured", and no method, year or count accompanies the statement [112]. The only published inputs date from 2018, when the program had moved 20 people out of the jail and the cost of holding an inmate with mental illness was put at $70 a day [27].

Two habits help when reading the figures. A count of people served or admitted shows that the door was open; it carries no county of residence, so it says nothing about where people came from [64]. And the fifteen-county list is an assignment [3]. The state's evaluators reported that crisis centers may serve fewer people from communities outside the metropolitan areas where they sit [62].
