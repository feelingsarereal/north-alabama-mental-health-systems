## What it is

A Continuum of Care is a regional body, recognized by the federal housing department, that plans the local response to homelessness [397]. Despite the name it is an organization and not a range of services, and it should not be confused with the continuum of mental health care described in Domain 3 [397]. Each continuum coordinates housing and services for homeless people in its area, runs the point-in-time count and sets priorities for federal Continuum of Care grants [397]. Alabama has nine, which together cover the state [397].

The point-in-time count is a count of everyone found homeless, in shelters and outside, on a single night in January [397]. The federal report of each count sorts people by where they were that night (emergency shelter, transitional housing or unsheltered) and adds lines for subgroups, such as people recorded as severely mentally ill or as chronically homeless [60]. It is a snapshot [397]. People who are homeless at other times of year, or who are staying with friends, are not in it, and the result depends on the weather and on how many volunteers search [397].

The housing inventory is the companion document: a list of the beds in the continuum, with a summary table and a project-by-project list [61]. It groups year-round beds as emergency shelter, transitional housing, permanent supportive housing and rapid re-housing [61]. Permanent supportive housing is the housing agencies' term for an ordinary home paired with visiting support services, when it is aimed at people leaving homelessness [397].

## How it works in north Alabama

WellStone's two counties belong to different homelessness systems [311] [312]. Madison County is one of three counties, with Limestone and Morgan, in the continuum run by the North Alabama Coalition for the Homeless, numbered AL-503 [311]. Cullman County is one of 37 in the Alabama Balance of State continuum, coordinated from Montgomery [312]. Federal counts, inventories and grants follow those lines, so each Huntsville figure below covers three counties [311]. The fifteen counties of the crisis catchment are split among four continuums [311].

The federal reports give 549 people in the Huntsville continuum in 2022, 646 in 2023, 621 in 2024 and 718 in 2025 [59] [313] [314] [60]. The 2022 count was taken on 22 February; the other three were taken in late January [59] [313] [314] [60]. Of the 718 people counted in January 2025, 416 were in emergency shelter, 37 in transitional housing and 265 unsheltered [60]. The inventory for the same month lists 421 year-round emergency shelter beds [61]. No 2026 count had been published by the federal department or the coalition by October 2026 [315].

The inventory tells the other half of the story. Permanent supportive housing stood at 334 beds in 2022, 154 in 2023, 153 in 2024 and 190 in 2025 [61]. In 2022, 268 of the 334 beds were in a veterans' voucher project [316]. The 2025 inventory names two projects: the Huntsville Housing Authority's, with 166 beds, and WellStone's Shelter Plus Care, with 24 [61]. That January the count found 128 chronically homeless people [60]. The inventory lists beds; the rows read for this edition do not say how many were free [61]. Federal continuum grants for FY2024 came to $945,663, the largest of them $615,231 to the housing authority for supportive housing [317].

The subgroup lines are less steady than the totals. The number recorded as severely mentally ill was 71 in 2022, 24 in 2023, 40 in 2024 and 49 in 2025 [313] [60]. In 2023 none of the people recorded as severely mentally ill was listed as unsheltered [313]. The chronically homeless line ran 118, 218, 71 and 128 over the four reports, and the line for chronic substance use fell from 117 to 41 [59] [60]. The report reads the 2023 figure as saying more about how the count was taken than about who was outside [313].

For Cullman County there is no count [210]. The Balance of State continuum reported 636 people across its 37 counties on 27 January 2026, 72.3% of them unsheltered, with no county breakdown [210]. The only project with Cullman in its name in the 2025 federal inventory for that continuum is a 21-bed domestic violence shelter [323]. Emergency shelter in the county depends on the weather: the city's warming center may open when the forecast is 28 degrees or below [324].

## How this report uses it

Section 11 gives most of its length to housing, and the same figures reappear in the summary in Part I, in the dependency map and in Domain 6's table of what is not published. From them the report computes that supportive housing in the Huntsville continuum fell by more than two-fifths in three years while the count of homeless people rose by almost a third [61] [60] [59].

Three cautions apply when reading those figures. The first is geography: a Huntsville figure is a three-county figure, and no count for Madison County alone or for Cullman County was found [311] [210]. The second is the nature of a one-night count, which the statewide report describes as better for rough scale than for year-to-year change [397]. The third is the reading of the tables. In the 2025 inventory the supportive housing row shows 24 beds in one subgroup column and 166 in the next, and readings disagreed on which column holds beds for chronically homeless people and which holds veterans' beds [61]. The year-round totals agreed on every reading [61]. The table is on the list of items that need a check by eye, and the open items add a second question: whether the housing authority's 166-bed project is the veterans' voucher program that held 268 beds in 2022 [61] [316].

The report also declines to treat the mental illness line of the count as a series [313]. For Cullman County it can say only that no county count was found [210].
