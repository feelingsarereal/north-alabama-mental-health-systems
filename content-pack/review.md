# About this report

Prepared for Little Orange Fish, Huntsville, Alabama, the backbone organization for the North Alabama Mental Health Coalition [397], as the regional companion to its *Alabama Mental Health Systems Report*. The statewide report describes the system from Montgomery outward. This one starts in two counties, Madison and Cullman, and asks the same questions of the institutions a resident there would actually meet. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0001 -->

**How to read this report.** Part I is a short guide to the region and to the three different maps that are all called WellStone's service area. Part II works through the eight domains used in the statewide report, each applied to Madison and Cullman counties. Part III adds the four sections on systems outside the Department of Mental Health: children and families, justice, housing and social determinants, and payers. Part IV describes the thirteen surrounding counties whose crises route to Huntsville, sets out how the parts depend on one another, and lists what could not be read, confirmed or found. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0002 -->

**Status and intended use.** This is a draft for review. It has not been approved by Little Orange Fish and has not been peer reviewed. It is independent of WellStone and of every other organization it describes: none of them supplied material, saw the text or endorsed it, and nothing here is a position of the North Alabama Mental Health Coalition or of any member organization. It contains no clinical guidance and no political advocacy. Where it describes a policy choice, it describes it as the public record does. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0003 -->

**A note on method and AI.** The research and writing were done with Claude on 4 October 2026, working only from public sources: agency pages and documents, budgets, court records, organizations' own published reports and data, and local press where no primary document could be opened. Each statement of fact was recorded with the passage that supports it before any prose was written, and the bracketed numbers in the text lead to those sources. The sources were read through an automated reader that returns extracts, so quoted passages are the reader's rendering of the document and a few tables need checking by eye; those are listed in Appendix C. WellStone's own website refused the reader for all but a handful of pages, which is why much of what is said about its programs rests on the Department of Mental Health's documents and on press reports. Sentences marked as analysis are the report's own reading of the evidence. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0004 -->

# Part I. A Guide to the Region

<!-- section: guide-why -->
## Why a regional edition

The statewide report describes itself as a baseline for "a planned North Alabama regional edition built on the same template" [397]. This is that edition. The reason for writing it is simple enough: a state figure for Alabama is an average over 67 counties, and the two counties described here differ sharply from each other on most of the measures that matter. A reader who knows only where Alabama ranks learns little about Madison County, and less about the rural county to its south. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0005 -->

The region also has something the state as a whole lacks, which is a single organization at the center of the public system. WellStone is the community mental health center for both counties, and its own strategic plan names the residents of Madison and Cullman as its catchment [1] [2]. It runs the area's crisis center, its 988 call center and a pediatric crisis unit described at its opening as the first of its kind in the state [3] [4] [122]. A report on the region is therefore partly a report on one provider. It is written as a description of a system of systems all the same, because most of what decides whether a person gets help, keeps housing or stays out of jail is decided somewhere else: in a probate court, a hospital emergency room, a school, a city budget. <!-- b: B0006 -->

<!-- section: guide-three-maps -->
## One service area, three maps

Ask what WellStone's service area is and the public record gives three answers. <!-- b: B0007 -->

The first is two counties. The Department of Mental Health's county-by-county emergency list assigns Madison and Cullman, and only those two, to WellStone as their community mental health center [2]. The Department's directory of Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics lists full-scope clinic sites at WellStone's Huntsville and Cullman campuses [5]. Together the two counties had 527,525 residents in the Census Bureau's July 2025 estimates, a sum this report computed from the county figures [6]. <!-- b: B0008 -->

The second is fifteen. The Department's Crisis Centers page lists fifteen counties under "Serving" for WellStone Emergency Services and WellStone Pediatric Crisis Care, at one address on Memorial Parkway in Huntsville: Madison and Cullman, plus Cherokee, DeKalb, Etowah, Fayette, Jackson, Lamar, Lawrence, Limestone, Marion, Marshall, Morgan, Walker and Winston [3]. Two other Department pages give the same list, one of them describing the thirteen as "surrounding counties" [7] [8]. For routine care those thirteen belong to four other community mental health centers [2]. Summed from the same Census estimates, they hold 792,981 people, so three of every five residents of the crisis catchment live outside the two counties whose local governments appoint WellStone's board [6] [1]. The catchment's southern edge meets the counties assigned to the Birmingham and Tuscaloosa crisis centers, and to its west lie Colbert, Franklin and Lauderdale, three of the seven Alabama counties the Department's own map shows with no crisis center at all [3] [7]. <!-- b: B0009 -->

The third answer is "Region 1," which turns out to mean three different things. The Department's mental illness services map draws a Region 1 of twenty counties and seven centers; its county membership is printed only in the map image, and this report relies on the statewide edition's reading of it [9]. The Department's Regional Offices page lists a Region I Community Services office in Decatur covering thirteen counties, which include Madison and Cullman but leave Fayette, Lamar, Marion, Walker and Winston to a Region II office in Tuscaloosa [10]. And the crisis intervention training program run by NAMI Alabama has its own Region 1 of eighteen north Alabama counties, with Huntsville Police as its training hub and WellStone Emergency Services as its crisis center [11]. <!-- b: B0010 -->

None of this is unusual for a state that built its regional lines one program at a time, but it has a practical consequence. The fifteen counties are not a unit in any of the other regional schemes read for this edition. They fall into three public health districts [12] and are answered by more than one 2-1-1 center: Huntsville's takes calls from Madison and five of the ring counties, while Cullman's calls are answered from Birmingham [13]. Even Madison and Cullman, which share a mental health center, belong to different councils of governments, different homelessness continuums and different 2-1-1 centers [395] [311] [13]. No Department page and no press report read for this edition lists the counties the Huntsville 988 center answers, though WellStone has said that Cullman callers reach it [4] [14] [41]. Throughout this report, "the core" means Madison and Cullman and "the ring" means the other thirteen; Region 1 is used only as a benchmark and always with its size stated. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0011 -->

<!-- section: guide-two-counties -->
## Two counties side by side

Madison County had an estimated 433,516 residents on 1 July 2025, up 11.7% since the 2020 census base, and Cullman County had 94,009, up 7.0% [15] [16]. Huntsville alone, at 233,627, is two and a half times the size of Cullman County; the city of Madison grew 19.4% in five years to 67,968, and the city of Cullman 13.4% to 20,799 [17] [18] [19]. In 2020 Madison County had 484.2 people per square mile and Cullman 119.6 [15] [16]. County Health Rankings counts 17.1% of Madison's population as living in a rural area and 75.6% of Cullman's [20]. <!-- b: B0012 -->

The people are different too. By race alone Madison County is 65.8% White and 24.6% Black; Cullman County is 93.0% White and 1.6% Black [15] [16]. Hispanic or Latino residents are 7.6% of Madison and 5.8% of Cullman, more than three times Cullman's Black share [15] [16]. Madison has 34,400 veterans to Cullman's 4,727 [15] [16]. Cullman is older, with 20.0% of residents 65 or over against Madison's 17.0%, and far fewer of its adults hold a bachelor's degree: 19.7% against 46.4% [16] [15]. <!-- b: B0013 -->

Money follows the same line. Median household income is $86,499 in Madison County and $62,656 in Cullman, poverty 9.6% and 14.1%, and the share of people under 65 with no health insurance 8.3% and 11.9% [15] [16]. One in ten Madison residents under 65 has a disability; in Cullman it is 13.5% [15] [16]. <!-- b: B0014 -->

It would be easy to stop there, with a prosperous metro county and a struggling rural one. The city figures spoil that picture. Inside Madison County, median household income is $134,655 in the city of Madison and $74,714 in Huntsville; poverty is 4.3% in one and 12.6% in the other, and Huntsville's uninsured rate of 10.6% is closer to Cullman County's 11.9% than to its own suburb's 4.4% [18] [17] [16]. The city of Cullman, for its part, has a poverty rate of 19.9%, the highest of the five places compared [19]. The gap between the two counties is real, and the gap inside Madison County is wider. That is the reason the regional map in this edition goes below the county line. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0015 -->

## What the evidence shows, in brief

1. **One service area is really three.** WellStone is the mental health center for two counties and the crisis center for fifteen, and none of the other regional boundaries read for this edition matches either [2] [3]. <!-- b: B0018 -->

2. **The front door got faster.** The wait for a first evaluation at WellStone fell from 18 days to under one during 2025, the only measured change in access found for the region [50]. <!-- b: B0019 -->

3. **Crisis care was built in four years.** Mobile crisis teams, an adult crisis center that reports over 150 admissions a month and a pediatric crisis unit all date from 2020 or later, alongside a 988 call center [31] [33] [1] [43] [4]. <!-- b: B0020 -->

4. **The pressure moved downstream.** What WellStone's plan and the probate court now ask for is stabilization for committed adults, group homes and supervised housing; a probate court official put the wait for a group home at 100 days or more in May 2025 [1] [51]. <!-- b: B0021 -->

5. **Cullman carries the heavier burden with fewer services.** Its suicide rate is 20.7 per 100,000 against Madison's 15.3; its hospital has no inpatient psychiatric unit, and no drop-in center or NAMI affiliate is listed there [52] [53] [54] [55]. <!-- b: B0022 -->

6. **The workforce shortage shows as beds that cannot be staffed.** A month after it opened, the pediatric unit could staff six of its 24 beds, and nursing is one of WellStone's three named unmet needs [56] [1]. <!-- b: B0023 -->

7. **The clinic payment model pays for Medicaid patients only.** The state requires a certified clinic to serve everyone and pays its daily rate only for Medicaid enrollees. In Madison County 18% of residents were eligible for Medicaid at some point in FY2024 [373] [57] [58]. <!-- b: B0024 -->

8. **Supportive housing fell as homelessness rose.** The Huntsville continuum counted 549 homeless people in 2022 and 718 in 2025, while its permanent supportive housing went from 334 beds to 190 [59] [60] [61]. <!-- b: B0025 -->

9. **Diversion is the purpose, and no count of it was found.** No count has been published of people diverted from jail or the emergency room by the crisis center or the police co-responders [62] [63]. <!-- b: B0026 -->

10. **The federal grants behind two recent programs have reached the end of their periods.** Federal awards for the clinic model and for outpatient commitment ended in September and July 2026 [39] [37]. <!-- b: B0027 -->

11. **Most of what a planner needs is not published.** There is no county split of people served, no crisis outcome, no jail caseload, no return for WellStone after FY2021 [64] [62] [65] [66]. <!-- b: B0028 -->

# Part II. The Eight Domains

<!-- section: d1 -->
## Domain 1. Governance, Policy and Legal Foundations

<!-- section: d1-scope -->
### Scope

The statewide report describes who decides in Montgomery: the Department, the Legislature, the Governor and the federal courts. This section asks who decides locally. In Madison and Cullman counties that means one nonprofit board, five local governments, two probate courts and a handful of planning documents, and the question in each case is whom the body answers to and what it has put in writing. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0032 -->

<!-- section: d1-the-center-and-its-board -->
### The center and its board

Alabama delivers public mental health care through community mental health centers that the Department of Mental Health calls "the designated points of entry" for mental health and substance use services [21]. The centers are governed by boards formed under Act 310 of 1967, which provides for public corporations to be formed to contract with the Department [21]. WellStone is the 310 board for mental illness and substance use in both counties. It says it was incorporated in 1969 under that act, operated for more than 25 years as the Mental Health Center of Madison County, and took the WellStone name in 2014 [1] [23]. The Cullman Area Mental Health Authority merged into it on 1 October 2017, and Cullman's executive director became the combined organization's chief operating officer [26] [1]. <!-- b: B0033 -->

The board has eighteen members. Six are appointed by the City of Huntsville, six by the Madison County Commission, three by the City of Cullman and three by the Cullman County Commission [1]. The Cullman City Council's minutes show the mechanism at work: in March 2026 it reappointed a member for a term ending in 2032 [67]. The City of Madison, which had a $45,000 appropriation to WellStone on its council agenda for FY2024, appoints no one [1] [68]. Neither do the thirteen ring counties whose residents the Department assigns to the crisis center this board governs [3]. That is not an oversight in anyone's bylaws. The crisis center was added in 2021–22 to an organization incorporated in 1969 for one county and merged with a second in 2017, and nothing in the public record read for this edition shows the board's makeup being revisited. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0034 -->

WellStone states that it is certified by the Department, and the Department says it moved to the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic model in 2024 [1] [46]. Its chief executive is Jeremy Blair [69]. Its planning process, by its own account, takes input from the board, employees, a Human Rights Committee, clients and families, local government and partner agencies [1]. <!-- b: B0035 -->

Mental illness is one of three 310 plans on file for the two counties. The Madison County 310 Board, created by Huntsville and the County Commission in 1990, coordinates intellectual disability services, and the Cullman County 310 Authority does the same in Cullman [70] [71]. Both report local government funding without stating amounts [71] [72]. <!-- b: B0036 -->

<!-- section: d1-local-government -->
### What local government does

Local governments reach mental health mainly through their budgets and their police. Huntsville's council voted in May 2021 to fund clinicians who ride with its police crisis intervention officers, and the city describes a Homeless Services Team of peer specialists, social workers and officers [63] [73]. Its adopted FY2027 General Fund budget of $360,593,840 carries $14,788,543 for outside agencies, with another $17,015,229 for outside agencies from its lodging and liquor fund; the release names no agency, and the appropriations book that does could not be opened [74]. Domain 2 sets out what is known about the amounts. <!-- b: B0037 -->

Madison County's role shows most clearly in its jail. When the Commission approved its FY2027 budget in September 2026 it renewed the jail's health care agreement with Southern Health Partners, at an annualized base cost of about $3.74 million for up to 1,235 inmates, and added a mental health nurse paid from opioid settlement funds [75]. In Cullman, the County Commission agreed in 2018 to support the Stepping Up Initiative and authorized the sheriff to sign an agreement with WellStone for services to people repeatedly jailed; WellStone withdrew its screening from the jail in 2019 and Cullman was again a Stepping Up site in 2020–21; no source read says whether the arrangement continues today [76] [291] [290]. The Cullman City Council's minutes for January to July 2026 that mention WellStone or mental health record a board appointment, an event permit and a resident's comment, and no appropriation; a press account of the city's FY2027 budget lists a community service contract with WellStone and gives no amount [67] [100]. <!-- b: B0038 -->

Cullman County voters have also been asked directly to pay for mental health. A local amendment on the November 2024 ballot would have added a 15% tax on medical cannabis sales, with a third of the revenue going to the county's legislative delegation for mental health. It failed, 19,660 to 18,569 [44]. <!-- b: B0039 -->

<!-- section: d1-commitment-and-the-courts -->
### Commitment and the courts

Involuntary commitment is a probate court matter in Alabama, and the two courts differ sharply in how much they have built around it. Cullman County's probate court lists commitments among its case types and publishes no procedure, forms or counts [77]. In late 2023 the Cullman sheriff told the state's opioid settlement commission that the county no longer had facilities to take people who need mental evaluations [78]. <!-- b: B0040 -->

Madison County's court went further. It announced an assisted outpatient treatment program in partnership with WellStone, to start on 3 February 2020 and paid for from the passport application fees the court collects; a later account dates the program to late 2019 [30] [81]. A person enters it only after a commitment petition, an assessment, a physician's confirmation that outpatient care is appropriate, a written treatment plan and a hearing [30]. As designed, participants were to have a case manager and a therapist and to report every 30 days for up to 150 days [79]. The probate judge told a reporter in 2023 that the $35 passport fee funded two case managers, one for the patient and one for the family [80]. <!-- b: B0041 -->

The court then helped change state law. Its judge helped draft House Bill 70 of 2022, sponsored by a Huntsville representative, which added a ground for taking a person to a facility (being unable to meet medical and physical needs) and allowed courts to mandate outpatient care; it took effect in July 2022 [81] [36]. The judge has said the law lets the court move inpatients to outpatient status once they are stabilized [80]. <!-- b: B0042 -->

How much demand the court faces is known only from press reports. The probate judge said in February 2020 that over 300 people had been committed the previous year [79]. A January 2023 report gave 309 petitions in 2019 and 414 in 2021 [80]. A May 2025 television report said involuntary commitments peaked at 589 in 2023 and named no source for the figure [51]. These count different things (people, petitions, commitments) and should not be read as one series. The same 2025 report describes the process as it now runs: a person held on an intent to commit has a hearing at about the third or fourth day and by then is at Huntsville Hospital or WellStone; inpatient commitment lasts up to 150 days and can be renewed to 300, which the judge called very rare because of bed availability [51]. <!-- b: B0043 -->

<!-- figure: fig-commitments -->

On results the press reports disagree. One account, written the year the bill was drafted, said about 63 people had been through the outpatient program and about 10% had returned to the court, against nearly 50% of people committed in the traditional way [81]. The 2023 report gives repeat cases as 86 of 414 petitions in 2021 against 142 of 309 in 2019 [80]. No evaluation of the program has been published. <!-- b: B0044 -->

Cullman County has tried a specialty docket of its own. A mental health court for nonviolent offenders began in Cullman County in late May 2022 under the presiding circuit judge, with WellStone as a partner; participants plead guilty first and can have charges dismissed by meeting milestones the court sets [82]. Section 10 takes up the courts in detail. <!-- b: B0045 -->

<!-- section: d1-plans-on-file -->
### What is written down

Three planning documents describe the region's needs, and they do not agree on what matters most. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0046 -->

WellStone's FY2026–27 strategic plan, signed by its board president on 18 November 2025, names three unmet needs: adequate housing for clients, funding for medications for adults with no third-party reimbursement, and recruiting and retaining nurses [1]. Its priorities for expansion are crisis stabilization for adults under civil commitment, supervised housing, integration of behavioral and primary care, and satellite access points [1]. Its four goals, by contrast, are internal: financial stability, customer experience, business processes and the number of clinicians certified in evidence-based treatments. None sets a target for the population served or for an outcome [1]. <!-- b: B0047 -->

Cullman Regional Medical Center's 2025 community health needs assessment ranks mental health first and substance use second among thirteen areas of opportunity [83]. Of the 47 community representatives it surveyed, 62.2% rated mental health a major problem, and one wrote that there were "few resources for mental health treatment other than incarceration" [83]. The hospital's implementation plan, adopted on 11 March 2025, takes on mental health as one of seven needs and leaves substance use out [84]. Its strategies are to support partners, implement a suicide prevention program, continue its outpatient psychiatry clinic and keep a dedicated behavioral health area in its emergency room [84]. <!-- b: B0048 -->

In Madison County the nearest equivalent is the needs assessment United Way ran in mid-2024 with a team from Alabama A&M University [85]. Its executive summary lists transportation, pollution and recycling, and services for abused and neglected children as the three areas residents most often said needed improvement. Mental health is not among them, though the report recommends investment in crisis intervention, counseling and community-based support [86]. No community health needs assessment from Huntsville Hospital was found. <!-- b: B0049 -->

### Gaps and opportunities

- **Representation has not followed the catchment.** The board that governs a fifteen-county crisis center is appointed by four governments in two counties [1] [3]. No public document describes how the other thirteen counties, or their four mental health centers, take part in decisions about the center their residents are sent to. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0051 -->

- **The plan's goals are the organization's, and the needs are the community's.** WellStone's plan names housing, medication funding, nursing and commitment stabilization as needs and priorities, and then sets goals for financial stability, customer experience, business processes and clinician certification [1]. Nothing in the plan says who is responsible for the needs it names. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0052 -->

- **One court has built a program; neither publishes its numbers.** Madison County's outpatient commitment program is known mainly through press reports and a federal award record, and Cullman's court publishes no procedure, forms or counts for commitments [80] [37] [77]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0053 -->

- **Cullman's own assessment ranks substance use second, and its hospital's plan leaves it out** [83] [84]. <!-- b: B0054 -->

<!-- section: d2 -->
## Domain 2. Financing and Resource Allocation

<!-- section: d2-scope -->
### Scope

The statewide report follows the money from the Legislature to the Department. This section follows it the rest of the way: what reaches the two counties, from whom, and how much of that a resident could find out. The answer to the last question shapes the whole section. Capital spending is well documented because ribbon-cuttings are news. Operating money is visible only in fragments, and no Form 990 data for the organization at the center of the system appear in the public database for any year after FY2021 [66]. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0055 -->

<!-- section: d2-what-the-buildings-cost -->
### What the buildings cost

The crisis system in Huntsville began with a state decision. On 28 October 2020 the Department named WellStone one of the first three crisis center awardees, funded from an $18 million appropriation in the FY2021 General Fund budget [32]. The permanent building on South Memorial Parkway opened in September 2022 at a reported cost of $10 million: $3 million from the state, $2 million from the Madison County Commission, the City of Huntsville and the City of Madison together, and about $5 million left to a capital campaign called Be the Rock [87]. When the doors opened, the campaign had raised close to $550,000 [88]. Its web page publishes no goal, no total and no donor list, and in December 2024 WellStone's development director described it as winding down [89] [90]. <!-- b: B0056 -->

The pediatric unit followed. It cost $9 million, which WellStone credits to the Legislature, the Department and campaign donors without giving any of the three shares [91]. Madison County's federal pandemic recovery plan lists a $1,000,000 grant to WellStone under mental health services [92]. In Cullman, WellStone opened a renovated and enlarged building in April 2024; neither local paper reported what it cost or who paid [41]. A 16-bed residential detoxification program, River Crossing, opened on 24 June 2025 in a renovated facility, and the Department says its beds are funded by a grant from the state opioid settlement fund without printing the amount [45]. <!-- b: B0057 -->

The state's evaluation office gives the clearest picture of how the crisis center's own spending changed. Capital made up 74.5% of WellStone's crisis center expenditure in FY2021 and 78.6% in FY2022; by FY2024 operations and administration were 87.3% [62]. The same office says the state funds each of its six crisis centers at $7,000,000 a year [62]. The enacted FY2027 budget carries $36,000,000 for the "Existing Six Crisis Diversion Centers," which would be $6,000,000 apiece if divided evenly; neither document reconciles the two figures [93]. <!-- b: B0058 -->

<!-- section: d2-wellstones-books -->
### WellStone's books

The most recent year for which WellStone's Form 990 data are public is the one that ended in September 2021. It reported revenue of $25,940,539, expenses of $20,991,369 and net assets of $17,628,969 [66]. Revenue had been $14,013,123 in the last year before the Cullman merger and $19,585,170 in the first year after it [94]. For fiscal years 2022 through 2025 the public database shows single-audit records and no Form 990 data, and flags the FY2025 audit with a significant deficiency in internal controls; the audits themselves could not be opened [66]. <!-- b: B0059 -->

WellStone's own annual report for FY2021 is the only place found where it has broken revenue down by source: $9,882,565 from state government, $8,654,700 from patient fees, $2,639,254 from local government and $1,026,303 from the federal government, plus other revenue the extracted text does not show in full [95]. That year included local capital contributions to the crisis center, so it may not represent a normal one. The current strategic plan lists the same kinds of sources with no amounts at all [1]. <!-- b: B0060 -->

Federal grants are the exception, because the federal government publishes them. The Department of Health and Human Services' grants database shows three awards to WellStone between fiscal years 2021 and 2025, totaling $9,575,938 [96]. A two-year community mental health center grant awarded in 2021 at $4,288,700 was later reduced to $2,502,021 [97]. <!-- section: d3 -->
## Domain 3. The Service-Delivery Continuum

<!-- section: d3-scope -->
### Scope

This section follows the continuum the statewide report uses, from prevention through outpatient care, crisis response, inpatient treatment and recovery supports, and asks at each step what exists in Madison and Cullman counties and how much use it gets. The short version is that the front of the system changed a great deal between 2020 and 2025 and the back of it did not. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0072 -->

<!-- section: d3-who-was-served -->
### Who was served

The Department's annual reports count the people WellStone serves for mental illness: 9,301 in FY2021, 9,460 in FY2022, 9,795 in FY2023 and 9,700 in FY2024 [109] [110] [111] [43]. The FY2025 report gives 11,659, with a footnote saying adult mobile crisis services are now included [64]. That year was also WellStone's first full one as a Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic, a status the state certified in July 2024, and the report's clinic table gives 13,700 for WellStone out of 30,275 for the state's two clinics [42] [64]. One reading of that page attached a different number, 6,627, to WellStone's clinic row; the page prints each heading beneath its table, and 6,627 belongs to the 988 call center table above it. The table is listed in Appendix C for checking by eye. <!-- b: B0073 -->

WellStone's own figures for the same year are "over 12,000" in its strategic plan and "more than 13,500" in a May 2026 press account, and in 2024 it was describing 14,000 clients a year [1] [48] [112]. None of the sources divides its count between the two counties. On the Department's FY2025 figures, this report calculates that the center reached between 2.2% and 2.6% of the two counties' residents, depending on which count is used [64]. <!-- b: B0074 -->

Substance use is the one series that fell. The Department counted 1,248 people served by WellStone for substance use in FY2021 and 1,087 in FY2024, a decline in each year between [109] [43]. <!-- b: B0075 -->

<!-- section: d3-the-community-base -->
### The community base

WellStone delivers the publicly funded outpatient system in both counties. Its plan lists counseling, case management and intensive in-home teams for adults, the Nova Center for Youth & Family for children, crisis services and community education [1]. The Department's directory shows two sites offering the full range of clinic services, at 4040 Memorial Parkway SW in Huntsville and 1909 Commerce Avenue in Cullman [5]. In spring 2026 WellStone opened its first clinic in the city of Madison, offering individual, group and intensive outpatient therapy to adults and referring out for psychiatry, primary care and detox [48] [113]. In November 2025 it had put a van on the road, which it called the state's first mobile mental health clinic, starting at First Stop and the Downtown Rescue Mission [47]. It also runs NOVA-Huntsville, one of Alabama's three programs for a first episode of psychosis [114]. <!-- b: B0076 -->

The clearest measured change of the period is in how fast a new patient is seen. The Department told legislators in January 2026 that WellStone's wait for an initial evaluation fell from 18 days in January 2025 to less than one day in December, and the wait for a first therapy session from 33 days to eight [50]. These are two points in time for one clinic, reported without a method note, but no other measurement of access over time was found for the region. <!-- b: B0077 -->

<!-- figure: fig-access -->

<!-- section: d3-crisis-response -->
### Crisis response

The statewide report describes crisis care as someone to contact, someone to respond and a safe place to go. All three now exist in Huntsville, and the crisis center and the response teams were built after 2020. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0078 -->

**Someone to contact.** One of Alabama's four in-state 988 call centers is at WellStone [4]. The Department's FY2025 table gives it 6,627 of the 35,118 recorded for the four centers, without saying whether the unit is calls or people; the report's text counts 40,053 calls statewide, a difference it does not explain [64]. The Department's board was told in November 2025 that the state answer rate had been above 90% since July; no rate is published for Huntsville alone [115]. Crisis Services of North Alabama runs a separate 24-hour HELPline and manages 2-1-1 for Madison and five other north Alabama counties, Cullman not among them [116]. <!-- b: B0079 -->

**Someone to respond.** Mobile crisis started in Cullman, which got a state-funded adult team as a rural pilot announced in June 2020; a team member said in September 2022 that it had intervened in more than 150 crises that summer [31] [117]. Huntsville's adult team came later, with the 2022 federal grant [118] [119]. WellStone's child and adolescent teams cover both counties and served 498 young people in FY2025, more than half of the 936 served by all such teams in Alabama [120] [64]. Alongside these, the Huntsville City Council voted on 13 May 2021 to put two master's-level WellStone clinicians in patrol cars with crisis-trained officers, at a time when police said they answered 150 to 200 mental health calls a month [34]. Two clinicians were riding by February 2022 [63]. No count of what the co-responders have done since has been published. <!-- b: B0080 -->

**A safe place to go.** A temporary diversion center opened on WellStone's campus in May 2021; in February 2022 its chief executive described it as having ten inpatient and ten observation beds [33]. The permanent building, WellStone Emergency Services, had its ribbon cut on 19 September 2022: 25,000 square feet, built for up to 15 temporary observation beds and up to 24 extended observation beds, starting with 16 [38] [88]. At the temporary site the center had averaged 45 to 50 people a month [88]. By September 2024 the adult unit was admitting 175 a month, and WellStone's plan of November 2025 says "over 150" [121] [1]. The Department counts 1,879 people served there in FY2025, which is consistent with those monthly figures [64]. That count covers all fifteen counties of the catchment. <!-- b: B0081 -->

The pediatric unit, described at its opening as the first of its kind in the state, opened next door in late September 2024 with 24 beds for ages 12 to 18 [122] [43]. A month later a staffing shortage meant it could treat six children at a time [56]. It averaged about 25 patients a month through the end of 2024 [43]. For its first full year there are two numbers: the Department's count for FY2025 is 520, and a press report of February 2026 gave 400 individuals served since opening and said the unit had begun taking children as young as eight [64] [69]. At the opening, the Department's commissioner said the state probably needs two or three more such units [122]. <!-- b: B0082 -->

What none of these sources reports is what a planner would most want to know. The state evaluation office's 2025 report on the crisis centers gives no admissions, lengths of stay, law enforcement drop-offs or diversion figures for Huntsville [62]. <!-- b: B0083 -->

<!-- section: d3-inpatient-care -->
### Inpatient care

The region lost its state hospital a decade ago. Officials announced on 13 January 2015 that North Alabama Regional Hospital in Decatur, with 74 beds, would close by 30 June of that year; patients were to be served instead by hospital acute units, crisis stabilization units, intermediate care run by community centers, or Bryce Hospital in Tuscaloosa [24]. The Department's own report records that with the closure all remaining state hospitals were in Tuscaloosa [123]. <!-- b: B0084 -->

What remains is hospital-based and thinly documented. Huntsville Hospital describes inpatient units for adults and seniors, reached through medical clearance and an assessment in its emergency department, and publishes no bed count [124]. Its parent system's audited statements identify psychiatric beds in only one place: Decatur Morgan West, in the next county, licensed for 64 [125]. Unity Psychiatric Care in Huntsville has 20 beds for people 55 and older [126]. The hospital association's listing for Crestwood shows no psychiatric service, and its listing for Cullman Regional shows outpatient psychiatry only [127] [53]. Cullman County, in other words, has no inpatient psychiatric unit; its hospital has a dedicated behavioral health area in the emergency room, opened in 2022 [128]. A television report in May 2025 said sixty mental health beds are in Madison County and serve patients from across north Alabama, without saying which beds it counted [51]. The state's health planning rule places Madison, Morgan and Cullman in a ten-county North Region for psychiatric bed need and prints no inventory by facility [129]. <!-- b: B0085 -->

This is where the pressure now sits. WellStone's plan names crisis stabilization for adults under civil commitment as its first priority for expansion [1]. The probate court's director of mental health programs said in May 2025 that the wait for a group home is at least 100 days and that she wanted more beds for stabilization and a step toward group homes [51]. When the state spent opioid settlement money on civil commitment and crisis residential beds, none went to Madison or Cullman [45]. <!-- b: B0086 -->

<!-- section: d3-substance-use-treatment -->
### Substance use treatment

The Department's directory lists fourteen substance use entries in Madison County and six in Cullman, including an entry at each county's jail [130] [131]. WellStone's part includes entries in that directory and, since 24 June 2025, River Crossing, a 16-bed residential withdrawal management unit in Huntsville funded from the state opioid settlement [64] [45]. Private providers carry much of the rest: Bradford's Madison campus, for one, offers medical detox, residential treatment and medication-assisted treatment [132]. Several well-known local recovery programs, among them The Pathfinder, His Way and the Downtown Rescue Mission, do not appear in the Department's directory, which shows only that they are not in the directory, not that they do not operate [130]. <!-- b: B0087 -->

<!-- section: d3-prevention-and-recovery -->
### Prevention, recovery and continuity

Prevention is present and uncounted. WellStone held the Department's substance use prevention award for both counties in FY2024 [133]. Its Heads Up program has taught Mental Health First Aid at the University of Alabama in Huntsville since 2023, with about 100 faculty and staff trained by September 2026, which is the only local count of that training found [134]. NAMI's Madison County affiliate and Crisis Services of North Alabama both list prevention education and publish no numbers [135] [136]. <!-- b: B0088 -->

Recovery supports cluster in Huntsville. The Department lists five drop-in centers for people with mental illness in the state and one in north Alabama, Our Place on Max Luther Drive; it lists Huntsville as a location of the peer-run Recovery Organization of Support Specialists; neither has a Cullman site [54] [137]. Not One More Alabama offers family and recovery support from Huntsville [138]. WellStone's 2022 federal grant promised a peer "living room" offering walk-in support to 400 people, and no source says whether it opened [39]. <!-- b: B0089 -->

Continuity, the hand-off from one level of care to the next, is described and unmeasured. People leaving the crisis center were to be assigned a care manager, according to a report at its opening [139]. The federal clinic model requires 15 to 17 outcome measures, follow-up after hospitalization among them; the first demonstration year ended in mid-2025 and no result for WellStone has been published [42]. <!-- b: B0090 -->

<!-- section: d3-veterans -->
### Veterans and the military

Madison County's 34,400 veterans and the workforce of Redstone Arsenal have a parallel system that leans on the civilian one at its edges [15]. Fox Army Health Center's behavioral health department takes self-referrals on weekdays; when it cannot provide the care needed, and it gives a child psychiatrist as the example, patients are sent off post through TRICARE [140]. For after-hours emergencies its page tells patients to go to the nearest emergency room or to WellStone Emergency Services [141]. The VA lists three sites in Huntsville and none in Cullman; its Huntsville clinic offers outpatient mental health care and its Vet Center free counseling without VA enrollment [142] [143] [144]. WellStone's 2022 grant committed it to intensive community care for service members and veterans, with a target of 50 served by September 2023, and no result has been reported [39]. <!-- b: B0091 -->

<!-- section: d4 -->
## Domain 4. Workforce Development and Well-being

<!-- section: d4-scope -->
### Scope

The statewide report measures Alabama's shortage with federal designations and national ratios. Those tools work poorly at county scale, as this section shows, so it leans on a plainer kind of evidence: what local providers have said they cannot staff. It then looks at where the region's future clinicians are being trained. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0097 -->

<!-- section: d4-the-shortage-measured -->
### The shortage, measured three ways

Start with the federal designation. Cullman County is a mental health professional shortage area for its low-income population, with a score of 16 [52]. Madison County has no area designation at all, and is the only county in Alabama without one [52]. That does not mean Madison has enough clinicians. The designation counts only psychiatrists, and the threshold is a population of at least 30,000 for each one, or 20,000 where needs are unusually high [146] [147]. A low-income designation like Cullman's says something narrower: that too few providers take Medicaid and sliding-fee patients for the size of the low-income population [146]. A county with a medical school campus and a large private market can clear the bar while its uninsured residents wait. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0098 -->

The second measure is the provider ratio, and here two published numbers exist for each county, and a third for Cullman. County Health Rankings' March 2026 figures give 474 residents per mental health provider in Madison and 568 in Cullman, against 639 for Alabama [52]. The Kids Count county profiles print 500 and 609 for 2024 [148] [149]. Cullman Regional's needs assessment reports 132.0 providers per 100,000 residents in Cullman County, against 140.9 for the state and 312.5 for the country [83]. The Rankings and the Cullman assessment draw on the federal provider registry with different definitions of a provider, and the Kids Count profiles print no source or definition. The broadest of them comes with a warning from its own publisher: the registry may include providers who are no longer practicing or accepting new patients, which would overstate supply [150]. On the Rankings measure both counties look better than Alabama; on the hospital's measure Cullman is below the state and far below the nation. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0099 -->

The third measure is what people say. A participant in the Cullman assessment wrote that there are "never enough psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses" [83]. <!-- b: B0100 -->

<!-- section: d4-what-cannot-be-staffed -->
### What cannot be staffed

The most concrete evidence of shortage in the region is a building. WellStone's pediatric crisis unit opened in September 2024 with 24 beds. On 23 October it could treat six children at a time, and its medical director described the problem as "just finding the right staff that have that passion for working with youth who are in a crisis" [56]. No later report says when, or whether, the unit reached full staffing. <!-- b: B0101 -->

WellStone's strategic plan a year later names "recruiting and retaining a qualified nursing workforce" as one of its three greatest unmet needs, gives no vacancy or turnover figure, and includes an objective to reduce overtime cost [1]. Its chief executive said in 2022 that the field lacks the workforce "locally, statewide, or nationally to absorb the demand," and put the cause on reimbursement rates that leave community providers unable to pay competitive wages [87]. The hospital system across town said much the same of its own nurses in 2023 [151]. <!-- b: B0102 -->

The organization has grown regardless. Its chief executive recalled 200 employees in 2011; press reports give 450 in September 2022 and more than 600 by February 2026 [87] [69]. A workforce target in the plan concerns quality, not numbers: 75% of therapists certified in an evidence-based practice by 30 September 2026, with no baseline stated and no result yet published [1]. <!-- b: B0103 -->

<!-- section: d4-the-pipeline -->
### The pipeline

Huntsville now trains psychiatrists. The University of Alabama at Birmingham runs a four-year psychiatry residency at its Huntsville campus, approved for up to six residents a year, with Huntsville Hospital as its clinical site [152] [153]. Its page listed two classes in October 2026, six first-year and six second-year residents, which suggests the program is new [154]. The curriculum includes eight weeks of community psychiatry and four each of forensic and addiction psychiatry [155]. The American Medical Association's residency database also lists a second four-year psychiatry program in Huntsville under WellStone's own name, with six first-year positions. The entry shows 24 current residents, a figure that equals four full classes of six and may describe approved positions; whether it has residents in training could not be established [156]. <!-- b: B0104 -->

The other professions have local programs too. The University of Alabama in Huntsville offers a psychiatric mental health nurse practitioner track requiring 720 clinical hours [157]. Alabama A&M University offers bachelor's, master's and doctoral degrees in social work and a master's in counseling [158] [159]. In Cullman County, Wallace State in Hanceville trains practical and associate-degree nurses and says its numbers are limited by faculty and clinical placements [160]. None of these pages publishes how many graduates it produces or where they go to work. <!-- b: B0105 -->

Two parts of the workforce are trained outside the academic system. Peer specialists are certified by the Department, whose trainings as listed in autumn 2026 are virtual or held in Columbiana, with none listed in either county [161]. Police crisis intervention training is organized regionally, with Huntsville Police as the hub for an eighteen-county region [11]. The city said in 2020 that officers must take 16 hours of it, with WellStone teaching several modules [162]. <!-- b: B0106 -->

Federal loan repayment is tied to the shortage score of the site where a clinician works, which favors sites in Cullman County; in Madison County only a site with its own facility designation would qualify, and whether WellStone's sites hold one was not established [163] [52]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0107 -->

### Well-being and retention

The fire department's page and the ambulance service's home page describe staffing and equipment and say nothing about peer support or behavioral health for their own crews; the police department's directives sit in a portal the reader could not search [164] [165] [166]. Absence from a web page is not absence of a program. It does mean that a resident, or a job applicant, cannot find out. <!-- b: B0108 -->

<!-- section: d5 -->
## Domain 5. Medicines, Technology and Infrastructure

<!-- section: d5-scope -->
### Scope

This section covers the physical and technical base of the local system: the buildings, the record systems, the medicines, and the means of getting a person from where they are to where the care is. The last of these turns out to be the weakest. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0114 -->

<!-- section: d5-facilities -->
### Facilities

The region's crisis buildings stand together on South Memorial Parkway in Huntsville. The Department lists WellStone Emergency Services and WellStone Pediatric Crisis Care at one address, 4020 Memorial Parkway SW, and WellStone's Huntsville outpatient clinic is next door at 4040 [3] [5]. The adult center is a 25,000-square-foot building; the pediatric unit beside it is 12,000 square feet with 24 beds, and can hold a child for up to 14 days [88] [91] [167]. WellStone's website names eight service locations in all, including a Madison campus, the Nova Center for children, the River Crossing detox unit and a recovery center, though the pages that would give their addresses did not open [168]. <!-- b: B0115 -->

WellStone's Cullman campus is at 1909 Commerce Avenue NW. It was renovated and enlarged by about 3,000 square feet in April 2024, and it houses the county's mobile crisis teams, outreach and primary care [41] [145]. There is no crisis facility in the county. WellStone's chief operating officer said as much at a town hall in October 2024: Cullman has crisis response teams and no local crisis center, so a resident who needs one goes to Huntsville [169]. <!-- b: B0116 -->

Hospital capacity is large in total and small for psychiatry. Huntsville Hospital has 881 authorized beds and state approval, granted in November 2024, for a $150 million tower that will take it to 931 [170] [171]. Cullman Regional became a 175-bed hospital in January 2024 after a $30 million expansion, and employs a psychiatrist in an outpatient clinic with counselors there and in the emergency room [172] [84]. The health system that owns Huntsville Hospital describes Decatur Morgan West, in Morgan County, as north Alabama's only behavioral health hospital, with 64 licensed beds used mainly for children and adolescents [151]. Domain 3 sets out what is known about psychiatric beds; the short answer is that no public source read gives a full count by facility. <!-- b: B0117 -->

<!-- section: d5-information-systems -->
### Information systems

Little is published about how local providers keep and share records. The state requires every Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic to connect its electronic health record to Alabama's health information exchange, which reported more than 5.5 million hospital discharge messages from 46 hospitals in 2025 and has a Huntsville Hospital representative in its governance [173] [174]. No document confirms that WellStone's connection is live. The homeless services system runs on its own database, and the coalition that manages it was rebuilding its website in October 2026 and published no data [175]. The Department's 988 page says nothing about a registry of open crisis beds [4]. <!-- b: B0118 -->

One public directory a resident might consult is unreliable. A state family services directory lists WellStone's service area as "Cullman and Decatur Counties," and another entry in the same directory still describes the crisis center as due to open in the summer of 2022 [176] [177]. <!-- b: B0119 -->

<!-- section: d5-telehealth-and-broadband -->
### Telehealth and broadband

Census figures show 92.3% of Madison County households and 87.3% of Cullman's with a broadband subscription [52]. The state's clinic manual expects telehealth to be used as far as possible to support access [57]. What is actually delivered that way locally is not documented. The one account found dates from the school closures of spring 2020, when WellStone moved school-based therapy to phone and video [178]. Cullman Regional's mental health plan does not mention telehealth [84]. <!-- b: B0120 -->

<!-- section: d5-medicines -->
### Medicines

Another of the three greatest unmet needs named in WellStone's strategic plan is "funding for medications for our adult clients with no third-party reimbursement" [1]. The plan does not say how many clients that covers or how large the shortfall is. Around it sits a patchwork. The Community Free Clinic in Huntsville fills prescriptions for uninsured Madison County residents from its own dispensary; in Cullman, United Way's 2022–23 funding list describes the Good Samaritan Health Clinic as providing health care and prescriptions for uninsured, low-income residents; and Huntsville Hospital's community grants list medication assistance among the needs they address [179] [180] [181]. WellStone's mobile unit, announced in November 2025, was described as dispensing psychiatric medication as well as offering therapy [47]. <!-- b: B0121 -->

For opioid use disorder the picture is clearer. Both counties are among the sixteen in Alabama with a certified opioid treatment program [182]. Huntsville has two clinics dispensing methadone and buprenorphine, both accepting Medicaid and Medicare, and Cullman has one, on the same street as WellStone's campus [183] [184] [185]. WellStone is the certified provider of outpatient substance use treatment inside both county jails [186] [187]. <!-- b: B0122 -->

A naloxone figure was found for one county only. The state's opioid council reports 2,344 doses distributed in Madison County between November 2024 and October 2025, placing it among the highest counties, and the text of its report does not mention Cullman, which may appear only on the report's map [188]. Huntsville's ambulance service was reported to have given the drug about 380 times in the first half of 2024 [189]. <!-- b: B0123 -->

<!-- section: d5-transportation -->
### Transportation

Getting to care is easier in Huntsville than anywhere else in the region, and even there it has limits. City buses run until 9 p.m. on weekdays and 5 p.m. on Saturdays, with no Sunday service listed [190]. The city's paratransit service has carried the mental health center's clients since the 1990s and now provides up to 10,000 rides a year to WellStone's adult day treatment program, with three operators assigned to it [191]. Outside the city limits, Madison County's rural service runs on weekdays only and must be booked a day ahead [192]. <!-- b: B0124 -->

Cullman County's only public transit is a demand-response service [398]. With 37 vehicles, it runs Monday to Friday from 6:30 a.m. to 2:30 p.m., and a ride must be scheduled by 2:30 the afternoon before [193] [192]. A person in Cullman who needs the crisis center in Huntsville on a weekday evening has no public way to get there. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0125 -->

That leaves ambulances and patrol cars. The Department itself says many rural residents cannot easily reach a crisis center, and offers mobile crisis teams as its answer [120]. For people under commitment, state law has the probate judge order "one or more persons or law enforcement officers" to take the person to hospital, with the expense taxed as a cost of the proceeding; another section has the state general fund pay such costs unless the party liable is not indigent [194] [195]. In Madison County the judge orders the sheriff to serve notice, and in 2020 he appointed deputies with more than 80 hours of crisis training as community mental health officers [196] [197]. For adults committed to a state bed the destination is Bryce Hospital in Tuscaloosa; no source read describes how often local deputies make that trip or what it costs them [198]. Before the pediatric unit opened, Huntsville's ambulance service was reported to be carrying about 800 children a year out of the county for mental health care [91]. <!-- b: B0126 -->

### Scope

### What is measured, and by whom

The most useful local figures come from state and national publishers, not local ones. ### Where the numbers disagree

Where figures do exist they often come in pairs. WellStone's FY2025 count is 11,659, 13,700, "over 12,000" or "more than 13,500," depending on the document; the first two are the Department's, from tables whose column headings were not captured, so their unit is unconfirmed [64] [1] [48]. ### Quality improvement

The clinic model brings reporting duties that should close part of this gap. <!-- section: d7 -->
## Domain 7. Equity, Inclusion and Cultural Responsiveness

<!-- section: d7-scope -->
### Scope

Part I set out who lives in the two counties. This section asks whether the system reaches them evenly: what the data show about differences between and within the counties, which groups have services built for them, and which do not appear in the record at all. The honest summary is that the region can describe its inequities by place and hardly at all by race, language or income, because the data needed for the second kind of description have not been published locally. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0147 -->

<!-- section: d7-what-the-data-show -->
### What the data show

By every measure this review found, Cullman County carries the heavier burden. Federal estimates put adult depression at 26.5% in Cullman and 22.0% in Madison in 2023, and frequent mental distress at 18.9% and 16.0% [211]. The suicide rate in County Health Rankings is 20.7 per 100,000 in Cullman and 15.3 in Madison; Cullman's own hospital assessment gives 20.6 for 2018–2022, against 16.5 for Alabama and 14.5 for the country [52] [83]. Drug overdose deaths run at 35.6 per 100,000 in Cullman and 29.1 in Madison [52]. Cullman has the federal shortage designation, fewer mental health providers for its population (568 residents per provider against Madison's 474), the higher uninsured rate and the higher share of residents with a disability [52] [16]. <!-- b: B0148 -->

The people Cullman Regional surveyed described the consequences in their own words. One wrote of "few resources for mental health treatment other than incarceration," and others named stigma and lack of insurance among the reasons people go without care [83]. Their assessment rests on 47 community representatives and secondary data, and does not break its mental health findings down by income, age, sex or Hispanic origin [83]. <!-- b: B0149 -->

Madison County's averages are better, and its volumes are larger. The county coroner's figure for 2022, as relayed in a television report, was one suicide every six days [206]. WellStone's emergency services director said in late 2023 that 55% of people who walked into the crisis center that year were experiencing suicidal thoughts [206]. And as Part I showed, the county's averages conceal a spread between Huntsville and the city of Madison that is wider than the gap between Madison and Cullman counties [18] [17]. <!-- b: B0150 -->

What cannot be said is how any of this differs by race. Madison County is a quarter Black and Huntsville nearly 30% [15] [17]. The state health department counted 834 suicides among Alabama residents in 2024 and publishes them by race and sex for every county, in a document whose Madison and Cullman pages the reader could not reach [212]. No local body publishes service use, crisis admissions, commitments or jail mental health caseloads by race. <!-- b: B0151 -->

<!-- section: d7-specific-communities -->
### Programs built for specific communities

**Veterans and military families** are the best served group, at least in Huntsville. The VA clinic offers individual and group therapy and the Vet Center counsels without requiring VA enrollment; the Vet Center's page lists no access point in Cullman, where 4,727 veterans live [143] [144] [15] [16]. A firearm-storage initiative for veterans and first responders runs with a Huntsville partner and reports that 86.4% of Alabama veteran suicides involve a firearm, against 73.5% nationally [213]. <!-- b: B0152 -->

**People with intellectual and developmental disabilities** have a separate and larger provider network than people with mental illness. The Department's directory lists 26 developmental disability entries in Madison County out of 43 of all kinds, and three in Cullman [130] [131]. The Arc of Madison County serves more than 315 people and runs an autism clinic [214]. The University of Alabama in Huntsville hosts the autism information hub for thirteen north Alabama counties [215]. Statewide, the waiting list for the Department's disability waivers stood at 1,800 people in May 2025, with no county figure [42]. <!-- b: B0153 -->

**Older adults** have dedicated inpatient care in Huntsville: a separate program for people 65 and over at Huntsville Hospital and 20 beds at Unity Psychiatric Care for people 55 and over [124] [126]. The two counties are served by different aging agencies [395]. Cullman's, based in Decatur, helps Medicare beneficiaries apply for benefits that cover premiums and prescription costs, and United Way of Cullman County's 2022–23 funded list included no behavioral health service for older people [216] [180]. <!-- b: B0154 -->

**LGBTQ+ residents** lost a dedicated counseling organization in Huntsville, Free2Be, which closed suddenly some years ago [217]. Thrive Alabama lists LGBTQ+ health care among its services in Huntsville [218]. Rocket City Pride's resource page names one counseling provider, a private practice, and does not list WellStone, Crisis Services of North Alabama or 988; a local television station's resource list has nothing in Cullman County [219] [220]. <!-- b: B0155 -->

**Hispanic residents** are 7.6% of Madison County and 5.8% of Cullman, and almost invisible in the service record [15] [16]. The statewide Hispanic services nonprofit lists one office, in Birmingham [221]. The state's clinic manual requires WellStone to have interpretation and translation available in proportion to its population with limited English, and nothing read says how it does so [57]. No local source describes Hispanic residents' use of, or barriers to, mental health care in either county. <!-- b: B0156 -->

**Deaf residents** are served through regional clinicians of the Department's Office of Deaf Services, whose page names no Huntsville staff [222]. **Students** have counseling centers at the University of Alabama in Huntsville and Alabama A&M, the latter with an on-call counselor after hours [223] [224]. <!-- b: B0157 -->

<!-- section: d7-gaps-and-opportunities -->
### Gaps and opportunities

- **The burden is heavier where the services are thinner.** Cullman has the higher rates of depression, suicide and overdose and the fewer providers, with no inpatient unit at its hospital and no VA site or peer recovery center listed there [211] [52] [53] [142] [231]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0158 -->

- **Nothing local is published by race.** In a county that is a quarter Black, no public figure shows who uses the crisis center, who is committed or who is jailed with a mental illness [15]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0159 -->

- **Language access is a rule without a report** [57]. <!-- b: B0160 -->

- **Community resource lists do not point to the public system.** The pride organization's list omits the crisis center and 988, which suggests the system's front door is not known, or not trusted, by everyone it is meant for [219]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0161 -->

## Domain 8. Community Engagement, Education and Advocacy

<!-- section: d8-scope -->
### Scope

This section describes who speaks for mental health in the two counties, how the public is educated about it, what local advocacy has won, and whether people who have lived with mental illness or addiction have a formal voice in how services are run. Little Orange Fish, the publisher of this report, is the backbone organization of one of the bodies described here, the North Alabama Mental Health Coalition; the coalition is described only from public web pages, its own and its founding organization's, and none of its meeting records were used as sources. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0162 -->

<!-- section: d8-who-engages -->
### Who engages

Madison County has a dense advocacy network. The North Alabama Mental Health Coalition was formed in 2015 by a committee of the Society of St. Vincent de Paul to improve the situation of people with serious mental illness, many of them homeless or in jail [25]. It describes itself as stakeholders who pool knowledge and resources to find and close gaps in access to care, and lists as core partners WellStone, Huntsville Hospital and Decatur West, the sheriff, the Huntsville and Madison police, the ambulance service, the VA, First Stop, the city, the county and the probate judges [225] [226]. <!-- b: B0163 -->

NAMI Madison County runs family and peer support groups, one of which meets in Athens in the next county, and an eight-week course for families [135] [227]. Crisis Services of North Alabama operates the HELPline, a domestic violence shelter and forensic nursing across four counties, with sexual assault response in Cullman and Winston as well [136]. Not One More Alabama, founded in 2016, offers family, grief and recovery support to people affected by addiction, and the Partnership for a Drug-Free Community runs prevention education, drug take-back and a recovery hub [228] [229]. The Alabama chapter of the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention holds its north Alabama walk in Huntsville [230]. <!-- b: B0164 -->

Cullman County has almost none of this. NAMI Alabama lists eleven affiliates and none in Cullman [55]. The Department's directory shows a peer-run recovery center in Huntsville and none in Cullman [231]. United Way of Cullman County names seventeen partner agencies, and no mental health treatment provider is among them; the closest in its 2022–23 funding list were a victim services agency, a sober living program and 2-1-1 [232] [180]. The organizations that do the work in Cullman are the hospital's partners: WellStone, Lighthouse Inc., The Link of Cullman County, and the police and sheriff [84]. <!-- b: B0165 -->

<!-- section: d8-public-education -->
### Public education

The best documented form of public education in the region is police training. An undated television feature describes Huntsville Police's crisis intervention certification as a 40-hour course of about 26 training blocks, taught by volunteers from NAMI and WellStone among others, with a 16-hour introduction for other personnel; the department is the training hub for an eighteen-county region [233] [234]. Mental Health First Aid is an eight-hour course that, the Department says, more than 90 certified instructors offer in Alabama; the Department publishes no count of people trained [235]. WellStone was announced to teach it free at the Cullman County library in July 2022 on a state grant [236]. A Huntsville television station has run a recurring "Mental Health Monday" segment, and the University of Alabama in Huntsville announced a suicide prevention campus walk for April 2025 [233] [237]. <!-- b: B0166 -->

<!-- section: d8-what-advocacy-achieved -->
### What advocacy has achieved

The region's recent gains can be dated, and several of them began locally. The probate court's assisted outpatient treatment program was launched publicly in February 2020, first paid for from the court's own passport fees [30]. The state named Huntsville one of three pilot crisis centers on 28 October 2020 [32]. The city council funded police co-responders in May 2021 [63]. A mental health court opened in Cullman in May 2022 [82]. A federal grant for outpatient commitment began in 2022, and the ribbon was cut on the pediatric crisis unit in September 2024 [80] [91]. The coalition claims a part in other changes, saying it spearheaded crisis intervention training locally and successfully pressed for mental health caseworkers in the Madison County jail [226]. Those are its own statements and were not independently confirmed. <!-- b: B0167 -->

The region also has unusual weight in Montgomery. The chair of the House General Fund budget committee, Rep. Rex Reynolds, is a Huntsville-area legislator, the same one who sponsored the 2022 commitment bill [238] [81]. In February 2025 he said that expanding mental health beds and crisis services would have to wait until prison construction costs were covered [238]. <!-- b: B0168 -->

A Cullman attempt to raise local money, a third of it for mental health, went to the voters and lost. Before the November 2024 referendum on a medical cannabis tax, Wallace State hosted a town hall at which WellStone's chief operating officer said his priorities, if the measure passed, would be residential and crisis care in Cullman, and a sheriff's captain said, "We don't have services here that we need" [169]. The measure failed by about 1,100 votes [44]. <!-- b: B0169 -->

<!-- section: d8-lived-experience -->
### The voice of lived experience

Peers lead much of the region's mutual support. NAMI's Connection groups are run by trained peers, the recovery center in Huntsville is staffed by people in recovery, and the Partnership's hub by certified peer specialists [227] [239] [229]. <!-- b: B0170 -->

Whether people with lived experience help govern the public system is harder to establish. Alabama's clinic manual requires that they and their families take a meaningful part in a clinic's governance, through a board at least 51% made up of such people or through another arrangement, and a clinic that uses the alternative must support the posting of an annual summary of that body's recommendations on its website [57]. WellStone's board is appointed by four local governments [1]. It has a Community Advisory Committee, known to this review only because the Madison County probate judge's biography says he sits on it; no membership list, remit or count of consumer and family seats was found [240]. <!-- b: B0171 -->

<!-- section: d8-gaps-and-opportunities -->
### Gaps and opportunities

- **Cullman has services, and no organized advocacy group was found there.** It has a WellStone campus and a hospital that ranks mental health first; no NAMI affiliate or peer recovery center is listed there, and no coalition of its own was found [242] [55] [231] [83]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0172 -->

- **The coalition's partner list is a Madison County list** [226]. The two counties share a mental health center and, as far as its public pages show, not a table. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0173 -->

- **A governance requirement that can be checked.** If WellStone meets the lived-experience rule through an advisory body, an annual summary of that body's recommendations should be on its website [57]. Its site was not checked for such a summary in this review. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0174 -->

- **No count of public education was found.** Apart from about 100 university staff, no figure was found for how many residents have had Mental Health First Aid [235] [134]. <!-- b: B0175 -->

# Part III. Systems Beyond the Department

<!-- section: s9 -->
## Section 9. Children, Youth and Families: A System of Care

<!-- section: s9-scope -->
### Scope and frame

A child's mental health care is delivered through more doors than an adult's: the school, the pediatrician, the child welfare agency, the juvenile court. The statewide report reads Alabama against the principles of a system of care, in which those doors open onto the same hallway. This section describes each door in Madison and Cullman counties and what is known about how many children pass through it. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0176 -->

<!-- section: s9-where-the-area-stands -->
### Where the area stands

About a quarter of each county's residents are children: 102,878 in Madison and 22,458 in Cullman in 2024 [148] [149]. Their circumstances differ in the way the adult figures would predict. In Madison County 13.1% of children lived in poverty in 2019–2023, the third-best rank in the state; in Cullman it was 18.5% [148] [149]. In Cullman 11.1% of teenagers were neither in school nor working, three times Madison's 3.7% [149]. <!-- b: B0177 -->

On the measures closest to mental health the picture is less tidy. Kids Count reports that 2,294 Madison County children and 444 Cullman children with serious emotional disturbance received mental health services in 2024, which this report calculates as roughly 2% of children in each county [148] [149]. Madison's schools suspended 11.1% of students in 2024–25 against Cullman's 3.3%, and recorded more of them as homeless [148] [149]. Preventable teen death rates were close, 70.9 and 73.0 per 100,000 in 2023, though Cullman's rests on four deaths [148] [149]. No county-level figure for youth suicide or self-harm is published. <!-- b: B0178 -->

<!-- section: s9-wellstones-childrens-services -->
### The public children's system

WellStone's children's services run through the Nova Center for Youth & Family in Huntsville and its Cullman campus [1] [241]. The Department's directory lists case management, outpatient care, a juvenile court liaison, an in-home intervention program and school-based services at the Cullman site [242]. Child and adolescent mobile crisis teams, funded by the state with the Department of Human Resources, cover both counties and saw 498 young people in FY2025 [120] [64]. No source gives the number of children WellStone serves in total. <!-- b: B0179 -->

The pediatric crisis unit is the largest change in children's services in the region for many years. Until it opened, WellStone said, Madison County had "no inpatient psychiatric options for youth" [91]. The nearest psychiatric hospital for adolescents was Decatur Morgan West in the next county, and the ambulance service was reported to be taking about 800 young people a year out of Madison County for stabilization, half to Decatur and half to Birmingham, Mobile, Dothan, Nashville or Chattanooga [112]. One family described waiting 24 hours in the emergency room and then traveling four hours for a bed [243]. <!-- b: B0180 -->

The ribbon was cut in September 2024 on a unit of 24 beds, with stays planned from under a day to two weeks, for ages 12 to 18 at first and from eight by early 2026 [91] [112] [69]. How much it has changed the flow of children out of the county cannot be measured from what is published. The Department's count for FY2025 is 520. A press report of February 2026 said the unit had served 400 individuals since opening, and WellStone's chief executive said it had passed its goal of reaching half of the "over 600" children formerly sent away [64] [69]. No source read for this report says how many children still leave. <!-- b: B0181 -->

What the unit is not is a hospital. It is a crisis service with a two-week ceiling, and no inpatient psychiatric unit for children was found in either county. Two residential programs appear in state lists: a 42-bed facility in Madison County on a 2022 education department list, and a 110-day program for boys aged 12 to 18 that the youth services department lists as serving Cullman among sixteen counties. Neither list describes a psychiatric role, and their current status was not checked [112] [405] [279] [267]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0182 -->

<!-- section: s9-schools -->
### Schools

Five school systems serve the two counties, and they have made different arrangements. Madison County Schools, with more than 20,500 students, names a mental health services coordinator, describes a three-tiered model with social workers and counselors, and lists three outside partners: WellStone's Nova Center, a Huntsville nonprofit called The Enrichment Center, and a grief program [244] [245]. Madison City Schools uses The Enrichment Center, not WellStone, for therapy on school grounds [246]. That nonprofit reports 35 school-based therapists, 1,290 students and 14,166 sessions in 2025–26 across three districts, at no cost to families and without billing insurance [247]. WellStone's therapists were reported to have seen Madison County students more than 1,700 times in FY2024 [248]. <!-- b: B0183 -->

Huntsville City Schools, which says it serves more than 23,000 students, could not be described: its counseling and student support pages returned only menus to the reader [249] [250]. In Cullman, the city schools employ a district mental health specialist and list three outside prevention programs, and the county schools had four full-time social workers and a coordinator in 2022 [251] [252] [253]. The Department lists WellStone's Cullman site as part of the state's school-based mental health collaboration without saying which district [242]. <!-- b: B0184 -->

Two details show how unevenly information travels. State law raised the age below which a student needs a parent's written consent for ongoing counseling from 14 to 16 in October 2025; Cullman City's page states the new rule and Madison County's still gives the old one [251] [254]. And the Department's list of which school systems partner with which mental health center returned an error on the day it was checked [255]. <!-- b: B0185 -->

<!-- section: s9-child-welfare -->
### Child welfare

Child welfare is where the two counties diverge most. Kids Count recorded 338 Cullman children with an indication of abuse or neglect in 2024, a rate of 16.4 per 1,000 that ranks 53rd of Alabama's 67 counties. Madison's rate was 3.6 per 1,000, the best in the state, against 10.1 statewide [149] [148] [256]. Foster care moved the other way: Madison County's count rose from 252 in 2014 to 411 in 2025, while Cullman's fell from 159 to 112 [148] [149]. <!-- b: B0186 -->

Huntsville is home to the National Children's Advocacy Center, which opened there in 1985 and provides services for Huntsville and Madison County [22] [401]. Its 2024 report gives 488 forensic interviews, 991 team case reviews and 129 forensic medical exams; its therapy figures for that year could not be read reliably and are left out [257]. It offers trauma therapy to children up to 18 regardless of a family's finances [258]. Cullman's center, Brooks' Place, provides forensic interviews, counseling and medical exams with five staff and says all its funding comes from local donations [259] [260]. Residential and foster care providers include Kids to Love in Madison, which also runs a trauma therapy center, and Childhaven in Cullman, with capacity for 32 [261] [262] [263]. <!-- b: B0187 -->

<!-- section: s9-juvenile-justice -->
### Juvenile justice

Madison County's juvenile court runs three specialty dockets: a drug court, a truancy court and an Alternative Court, which the circuit's website describes as founded by a judge to address the mental health needs of juveniles and as the first of its kind in Alabama [264] [265]. Its detention center, Neaves-Davis, has 48 beds; the county's page says nothing about mental health screening or services for the young people held there [266]. The state's youth services agency funds a 47-slot diversion program in the county with in-home crisis intervention and family counseling [267]. <!-- b: B0188 -->

Cullman County has a short-term juvenile detention facility of its own, renamed in October 2024, which does not appear on the state's list of eleven detention centers [268] [269] [270]. Its diversion grant has eight slots, and WellStone runs a 15-slot after-school program there [267]. Youth incarcerations have fallen in both counties: from 519 to 229 in Madison and from 116 to 78 in Cullman between the decade ending 2009 and the decade ending 2024 [148] [149]. <!-- b: B0189 -->

<!-- section: s9-early-childhood-and-pediatric-medicine -->
### Early childhood and pediatric medicine

The earliest years are covered by programs whose reach is counted and whose mental health content is not. Madison County had 1,563 children in First Class Pre-K and 766 in early intervention; Cullman had 314 in pre-K [148] [149]. The children's advocacy center made 397 home visits to first-time parents in 2024 [257]. The Department's infant and early childhood mental health page does not say which consultants serve either county [271]. One figure stands out: only 64.5% of Madison County births in 2023 followed adequate prenatal care, ranking it 62nd of 67, against 75.4% in Cullman [148]. Huntsville Hospital for Women & Children lists no postpartum or perinatal mood group among its support groups [272]. <!-- b: B0190 -->

Child psychiatry is scarce and its scarcity is undocumented. Huntsville Hospital for Women & Children says it runs the only dedicated pediatric emergency department in north Alabama, seeing more than 36,000 children a year; the system's pediatric page lists no pediatric psychiatry service [273] [274]. A university clinic in Huntsville that sees children as well as adults lists four psychiatrists, without saying which of them treat children [275]. One private child psychiatry practice states that it is at capacity and is not adding names to its waiting list [276]. The state's telephone consultation service for pediatricians said in 2024 that it can take three to six months to see a therapist or psychiatrist, a statewide statement [277]. For autism, a Huntsville clinic for children aged two to five gives a wait of three to four months, and the Department's guide lists 21 diagnostic providers for north Alabama families, none of them in Cullman County [278] [279]. <!-- b: B0191 -->

<!-- section: s10 -->
## Section 10. The Justice Interface

<!-- section: s10-scope -->
### Scope and frame

The statewide report organizes the meeting points between mental illness and the justice system by the Sequential Intercept Model: community and crisis services, law enforcement, initial detention and court, jails and courts, reentry, and community supervision. This section walks the same six intercepts in Madison and Cullman counties. It deals with deaths and lawsuits as well as programs. People who died or were charged are not named; cases are given by their captions, and what was alleged is kept apart from what a court found. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0197 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-0 -->
### Intercept 0: community and crisis response

Diversion was the stated purpose of the crisis center from the start. WellStone's chief executive described the beds in 2022 as a way to keep people out of jails and emergency departments, and the permanent building was designed with a rear entrance for people brought by ambulance or police [88]. The Department lists it as the place to which law enforcement and first responders in fifteen counties can bring a person in crisis at any hour [3]. <!-- b: B0198 -->

How many people it has diverted is unknown. WellStone's chief executive said in March 2024 that its jail diversion program was saving about $300,000 of taxpayer money a year "the last time we measured," and that the crisis center had produced savings for the emergency departments at Huntsville Hospital and Crestwood [112]. No method, year or count accompanies either statement. The only published inputs date from 2018, when the jail diversion program was a few months old: it had moved 20 people out of the jail, and the cost of holding an inmate with mental illness was put at $70 a day [27]. The state's own evaluation of the crisis centers in 2025 publishes no counts of jail or emergency room avoidance for any single center, found the centers' monthly reports inconsistent, and noted that one center, not named in the passages read, has always reported zero for jail avoidance [62]. <!-- b: B0199 -->

In Cullman the path to the crisis center runs through a phone line. As WellStone described it in 2024, a Cullman caller to 988 reaches the Huntsville center, which connects them to a local crisis team that can take them to Huntsville if needed [41]. <!-- b: B0200 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-1 -->
### Intercept 1: law enforcement

Huntsville Police have the region's most developed response. In May 2021 the department had 75 officers certified through the 40-hour crisis intervention course, and a later briefing gave 94 certified and 190 trained in the 16-hour version; the department's page publishes no current figure [34] [281] [209]. The co-responder program approved that month was planned for two clinicians working 2 p.m. to midnight, and WellStone's chief executive said a federal grant later added two more positions [34] [281]. The city's review of the department's 2025 does not mention co-responders, crisis intervention or mental health, and no count of calls attended, arrests avoided or transports was found on the City's or the department's pages in the program's five years [282] [63]. <!-- b: B0201 -->

Any account of how Huntsville police respond to people in crisis has to include one case. In 2018 a Huntsville officer shot and killed a man who was holding a gun to his own head [283]. The officer was convicted of murder in May 2021 and sentenced to 25 years that August [283]. On 24 March 2023 the Alabama Court of Criminal Appeals reversed the conviction over the jury instruction on self-defense, and on 13 October 2023 the former officer pleaded guilty to manslaughter, with three years' probation and a 20-year split sentence to take effect only on a probation violation, and there was no retrial [284] [283]. <!-- b: B0202 -->

Outside Huntsville the record thins. The Madison County Sheriff's Office, with 194 sworn deputy positions, lists mental commitments among the calls its patrol deputies handle and mentions no crisis intervention team [285]. In 2020 the probate judge appointed deputies with more than 80 hours of crisis training as community mental health officers [197]. Nothing on the Cullman County Sheriff's website shows crisis-trained officers or a co-responder, and the district attorney told a town hall in 2024 that the Sheriff's Office had handled hundreds of mental health calls in the previous year [286] [169]. <!-- b: B0203 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-2 -->
### Intercept 2: initial detention and first court appearance

Detention for Huntsville Police is run by the Madison County Sheriff's Office at the county jail, which makes the jail the booking point for the county's largest police department [287]. The county's Office of Alternative Sentencing and Release runs a Mental Health Pre-Trial Release Program there that interviews, evaluates and supervises detainees who show or have a diagnosis of mental illness; it publishes no numbers [288]. <!-- b: B0204 -->

The civil side of this intercept is the probate court, described in Domain 1. Its assisted outpatient treatment program is court-ordered care for people who can live safely in the community with treatment and have not stayed in it voluntarily [30]. The federal grant that has paid for it since 2022 set targets of 275 people enrolled over four years and a 75% decrease in participants needing inpatient care or incarceration; no results against those targets have been published, and the grant period ended in July 2026 [37]. <!-- b: B0205 -->

Huntsville's Municipal Court, which handles 45,000 to 50,000 cases a year with three judges, runs its own mental health court and a pretrial diversion program [289]. Its mental health docket requires defendants to attend counseling and see a psychologist and a psychiatrist [289]. For people without housing, the city's account is a judge's statement that the court tries to find them a place to live and the skills to stay there [289]. <!-- b: B0206 -->

In Cullman, as of the state's 2020–21 evaluation, WellStone staff evaluated people in the hospital emergency room on a probate judge's order or the hospital's request [290]. A 2019 arrangement let a judge order a mental health evaluation, adopted after voluntary screening in the jail failed: of 98 inmates the jail referred that year, four agreed to be screened [291] [28]. <!-- b: B0207 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-3 -->
### Intercept 3: jails and courts

Neither county publishes its jail's population or mental health caseload. The Madison County Sheriff's pages give no capacity or screening figures; the most recent capacity statement found is from 2010, when the then sheriff said the jail could hold about 1,200 [65] [292]. The health care contract approved in September 2026 covers up to 1,235 inmates at about $3.74 million a year and adds a mental health nurse paid from opioid settlement funds [75]. Cullman's detention center has 553 beds and a medical staff that includes a physician and nurses, with no mental health staff named; its contract with the same company, Southern Health Partners, is $849,156.24 for FY2027 [293] [294]. WellStone is the certified provider of outpatient substance use treatment inside both jails [186] [187]. <!-- b: B0208 -->

The Madison County jail's history includes three deaths in 2013 that led to federal lawsuits. The families' complaints, reported in October 2014, alleged that officers and medical staff failed to act as each person died of a treatable condition, and that the county and its then health contractor delayed hospital transfers to save money; the defendants denied breaching the standard of care [295]. The three cases, numbered 5:14-cv-01964, 5:14-cv-01959 and 5:14-cv-01309 in the Northern District of Alabama, were all reported as pending in September 2016 [296]. In the third, the court denied all motions to dismiss on 9 March 2015, a ruling that the complaint stated a claim and could proceed, not a finding that the allegations were proved [297]. How the three cases ended could not be established from any source that would open. The contractor named in the suits is no longer the jail's provider [298]. <!-- b: B0209 -->

Madison County has an Adult Mental Health Court. The circuit's site says a district judge has presided over it since 2015, and a circuit judge's biography says she has presided over it since taking the bench; the same circuit judge established a veterans court for veterans with mental illness or substance problems [299] [265]. The adult drug court is run from the county's alternative sentencing office [300]. None publishes its capacity or how many people have completed it. Cullman County's courts added a veterans court in May 2019 and a mental health court in May 2022, the latter with state funding and a federal grant and with WellStone providing services regardless of ability to pay [29] [35]. By October 2026 the circuit's web page named neither the judge who launched the mental health court nor any of the specialty dockets [301]. <!-- b: B0210 -->

Both counties were early sites for Stepping Up, the national initiative to reduce the number of people with mental illness in jails, and the results show how hard jail screening is to sustain. In the first year, 2018–19, Madison's site screened 82 inmates and found 35 positive, and the state's report said the county had no plan to sustain the work [28]. In 2020–21 the Madison jail referred 63 people, of whom 57 were confirmed as having a serious mental illness, 35 were referred on, and 12 people received case management, a figure that includes people carried over from earlier years; the state's report says no site summary was completed because of staff turnover and inconsistent data [290]. Cullman's numbers that year were 11 referred and three confirmed [290]. Whether either county is a current Stepping Up site could not be read from the Department's 2026 map [302]. <!-- b: B0211 -->

Competency to stand trial is the last piece. Jail-based restoration runs in seven Alabama counties, and neither Madison nor Cullman is one of them, so defendants found incompetent wait for the state's forensic hospital along with everyone else in a queue that stood at 273 men in August 2025 [64] [303]. No county-level wait is published. <!-- b: B0212 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-4-5 -->
### Intercepts 4 and 5: reentry and community supervision

Reentry support is better documented in the smaller county. The Link of Cullman County won a $500,000 federal Second Chance Act grant in 2020 for a reentry program of case management and follow-up, working with the sheriff and taking referrals from the state parole bureau [304] [305]. In Madison County, WellStone's jail diversion staff work to get people held because of mental health problems out of jail, into housing and to their follow-up appointments [112]. The parole bureau runs one of its five day reporting centers in Huntsville, for high-risk probationers and parolees, with mental health among the needs it addresses [306]. <!-- b: B0213 -->

Supervision in Madison County is concentrated in one office. Pretrial release, community corrections, misdemeanor probation, court referral and the drug court all sit in the Office of Alternative Sentencing and Release [288]. Cullman's community corrections program is a nonprofit based in the courthouse [307] [308]. Neither publishes how many of the people it supervises have a mental illness or what treatment conditions they carry. <!-- b: B0214 -->

<!-- section: s10-victims -->
### Victims and violence

The trauma side of the interface has its own agencies. Crisis Services of North Alabama serves victims of domestic and sexual violence in Madison and three neighboring counties, with a shelter and forensic exams, and provides sexual assault response in Cullman [136]. Cullman's domestic violence agency, renamed The Willow Path in September 2025, takes 600 to 800 crisis calls a year and has a 24-bed shelter [309] [310]. <!-- b: B0215 -->

### Gaps and opportunities

- **The most-cited success is the least measured.** Diversion is the reason the crisis center was built, and after four years there is no published count of people diverted, by either county's jail or either hospital [62] [112]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0217 -->

- **Programs are known by their launch dates.** The co-responders, Cullman's mental health and veterans courts and assisted outpatient treatment have a funding or launch date on the public record; the Madison County and municipal courts have not even that; none has an annual report [63] [299] [35] [29] [30]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0218 -->

- **What officials say is missing is a place to hold and treat.** The probate judge describes people who have "exhausted their circle of support"; the Cullman sheriff says the county has no facility for evaluations; WellStone's plan puts stabilization for committed adults first [36] [78] [1]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0219 -->

- **The jails are the system's least visible part.** Neither county publishes how many people in its jail have a mental illness, and the one state program that counted them recorded data problems in Madison and near-total refusal in Cullman [65] [290] [28]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0220 -->

<!-- section: s11 -->
## Section 11. Housing, Social Determinants and Community Supports

<!-- section: s11-scope -->
### Scope and frame

The statewide report reads Alabama against the five domains of social determinants used in Healthy People 2030: neighborhood and built environment, economic stability, health care access, education access, and social and community context. This section does the same for the two counties. It gives most of its length to housing, because housing is a need WellStone's own plan names twice and the one for which the public record is richest. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0221 -->

<!-- section: s11-housing-and-homelessness -->
### Neighborhood and built environment: housing and homelessness

The first fact to know is that WellStone's two counties belong to different homelessness systems. Madison County is one of three counties, with Limestone and Morgan, in the continuum of care run by the North Alabama Coalition for the Homeless [311]. Cullman County is one of 37 in the Alabama Balance of State continuum, coordinated from Montgomery [312]. Federal counts, funding and housing inventories follow those lines, so almost everything below describes Madison's three-county continuum, and almost nothing can be said about Cullman. <!-- b: B0222 -->

The count in the Huntsville continuum has risen. The federal report of the January 2025 count gives 718 people: 416 in emergency shelter, 37 in transitional housing and 265 unsheltered [60]. The figures for the three years before were 549, 646 and 621 [59] [313] [314]. On the night of the 2025 count the continuum had 421 year-round emergency shelter beds, nearly all of them full, and 265 people outside [61] [60]. No 2026 count had been published by either the federal department or the coalition by October 2026 [315]. <!-- b: B0223 -->

How many of those people have a serious mental illness is uncertain, because the published figure jumps about: 71 in 2022, 24 in 2023, 40 in 2024 and 49 in 2025 [313] [60]. In 2023 the report shows none of the people counted as severely mentally ill as unsheltered, which says more about how the count was taken than about who was outside [313]. The count of people with chronic substance use problems fell from 117 to 41 over the same four reports [60]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0224 -->

The housing that is meant to end homelessness for people with disabilities has shrunk. Permanent supportive housing in the continuum stood at 334 beds in the 2022 inventory, 154 in 2023, 153 in 2024 and 190 in 2025 [61]. In 2022 most of those beds, 268 of 334, were in a veterans' voucher project; the 2025 list has no project under that name, though the housing authority's 166-bed project, labelled "- V" in the list, may be the same program; that could not be confirmed [316] [61]. The 2025 inventory names two projects: the Huntsville Housing Authority's, with 166 beds, and WellStone's Shelter Plus Care, with 24 [61]. That month the count found 128 chronically homeless people [60]. Federal continuum funding for FY2024 came to $945,663 in four grants, the largest of them $615,231 to the housing authority for supportive housing [317]. <!-- b: B0225 -->

<!-- figure: fig-housing -->

Huntsville's city government leads the local response through its Community Development department [73]. It manages a sanctioned camp on South Derrick Street, rebuilt after a fire with marked spaces and a fence; the city's release of April 2025 says 84 spaces, while press reports in 2026 give a capacity of about 58 and some 30 to 35 residents [318] [319] [320]. People staying there must now take part in case management, and the city said in June 2026 that its Homeless Services Team had helped 22 former residents into stable housing in six months [320] [321]. First Stop, the day services provider, was tracking about 20 encampments across the county in March 2026 and thought that was about half of them [319]. The Downtown Rescue Mission supplies 200 of the continuum's emergency shelter beds and hosts its coordinated entry [61] [322]. <!-- b: B0226 -->

For Cullman there is no count. The Balance of State continuum reported 636 people across its 37 counties in January 2026, 72.3% of them unsheltered, with no county breakdown [210]. The only project with Cullman in its name in the 2025 federal housing inventory is a domestic violence shelter listed with 21 beds; the agency that runs it was reported in August 2026 to have 24 [323] [310]. Emergency shelter in the county is weather-triggered: the city's warming center may open when the forecast is 28 degrees or below [324]. The Cullman Housing Authority's voucher waiting list is closed and, it says, rarely opens [325]. <!-- b: B0227 -->

Behind all of this is the cost of ordinary housing. A two-bedroom apartment at fair market rent takes an hourly wage of $25.19 in the Huntsville area and $17.94 in Cullman County [326]. The Huntsville Housing Authority, with more than 1,500 rental units and close to 1,800 voucher families, reports a wait of six to twelve months for public housing [327] [328]. WellStone's plan names "adequate housing options to meet our clients' needs" and supervised housing for adult clients among its unmet needs [1]. <!-- b: B0228 -->

<!-- section: s11-economic-stability -->
### Economic stability

Work is plentiful in both counties by the usual measure: unemployment was 3.4% in Madison and 3.6% in Cullman in June 2026, after annual averages near 2.5% in 2025 [329] [330]. Income is another matter. County Health Rankings puts children in poverty at 13.5% in Madison and 21.3% in Cullman [20]. In 2023, 34,783 Madison County residents and 8,554 Cullman residents received food assistance through SNAP [331] [332]. In December 2024, 6,140 people in Madison and 2,241 in Cullman received Supplemental Security Income, nearly all on grounds of disability [333]. Set against population, Cullman's SSI count is proportionally the larger, which fits its higher disability rate [333] [334]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0229 -->

Employment support for people with serious mental illness is thin. None of the five mental health centers the Department named in February 2025 as offering Individual Placement and Support, the evidence-based supported employment model, is in north Alabama [335]. State vocational rehabilitation serves Madison County from Huntsville and Cullman County from Decatur [336]. <!-- b: B0230 -->

<!-- section: s11-health-care-access -->
### Health care access

Primary care is easier to find in Madison County: one physician for every 1,140 residents, against one for every 2,159 in Cullman [20]. The safety net is concentrated there too. A federally designated health center, HAPPI Health, lists four sites, all in Huntsville, with a sliding fee and its own behavioral health service of therapy, evaluations and crisis intervention [337] [338]. Thrive Alabama runs primary care, a behavioral health clinic and a pharmacy in Huntsville [339]. The Community Free Clinic has served uninsured Madison County residents since 1999 [340]. <!-- b: B0231 -->

Cullman has a free clinic. The Good Samaritan Health Clinic sees uninsured adults, provides most medications free through a patient assistance program, and offers mental health counseling through a partnership with a memorial fund; the page does not say who provides it [341] [342]. Neither county's health department lists a behavioral health service [343]. <!-- b: B0232 -->

<!-- section: s11-education-access -->
### Education access

School systems in both counties score above the state on Alabama's report card. In 2024–25 Cullman City scored 96 and Cullman County 91, with graduation rates of 96.83% and 96.96%; Madison City's campuses averaged 95, Madison County scored 91 and Huntsville City 89, against a state score of 87 [344] [345] [346] [403]. The gap opens after high school: 46.4% of Madison County adults hold a bachelor's degree and 19.7% of Cullman's [15]. <!-- b: B0233 -->

<!-- section: s11-social-and-community-context -->
### Social and community context

The two counties look alike on measures of social connection, with about a third of adults in each reporting loneliness [20]. They differ in the infrastructure around those adults. Madison County's 2-1-1 calls are answered in Huntsville and Cullman's from Birmingham [13]. The regional council that holds Madison County's aging agency lists mental health services among the things older people most need, and Cullman's commission on aging runs ten senior centers and delivers meals to almost 250 homebound people a day [347] [348]. Huntsville has a bus system and Cullman a demand-response service that averaged 190 rides per operating day in July 2026 [190] [398] [349]. <!-- b: B0234 -->

Community organizations carry much of the non-clinical load. In Madison County the public library keeps a guide to social services whose mental health page lists eight resources, and congregations coordinate emergency food through a shared line [350] [402] [351]. In Cullman, The Link's food pantry serves 800 households a month, Cullman Caring for Kids runs the United Way food bank, and The Link became an accredited family resource center in September 2026 [352] [180] [353]. <!-- b: B0235 -->

## Section 12. Payers, Coverage and the CCBHC Transition

<!-- section: s12-scope -->
### Scope

Who pays shapes who is served. This section sets out how residents of the two counties are covered, how Medicaid pays for mental health care here, what the move to the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic model has changed, and where the commercial and hospital markets stand. The statewide report explains the rules; this section applies them. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0242 -->

<!-- section: s12-the-coverage-picture -->
### The coverage picture

Madison County relies more on employer coverage than Cullman, where public programs cover a larger share. Census survey estimates for 2024 put 53.2% of Madison residents on employer plans, against 41.9% in Cullman; Medicaid covers 9.77% in Madison and 15.9% in Cullman, and Medicare 10.4% and 15% [354] [355]. Madison County is also home to 34,400 veterans [356]. The uninsured share was 7.72% in Madison and 10.9% in Cullman on those all-ages estimates, and 8.3% and 11.9% among people under 65 [354] [355] [356] [357]. <!-- b: B0243 -->

Administrative counts tell the same story from the other side. Alabama Medicaid counted 54,803 eligible people in Madison County and 15,922 in Cullman in April 2026, both down from a year earlier [358]. The agency reports that 18% of Madison's population was eligible at some point in FY2024, one of the five lowest shares in the state [58]. Medicare had 78,966 beneficiaries in Madison and 21,317 in Cullman in 2025; 9,547 and 4,141 of them were also eligible for Medicaid [359] [360]. The children's insurance program covered 12,741 children in Madison and 4,757 in Cullman in July 2026, up from 11,337 and 4,462 a year before [361] [362]. <!-- b: B0244 -->

One part of the picture changed sharply in 2026. After the enhanced marketplace tax credits expired at the end of 2025, about 95,000 Alabamians, some 23% of the state's enrollees, left the marketplace [363]. A hospital president in the Huntsville system had estimated beforehand that about 19,500 of those expected to lose coverage lived in the system's north Alabama territory [364]. The federal government publishes county-level marketplace plan selections for 2026, but the file could not be opened for this report, and no local estimate of the coverage gap in a state that has not expanded Medicaid could be found [365] [366]. <!-- b: B0245 -->

<!-- section: s12-medicaid-behavioral-health -->
### How Medicaid pays for mental health here

Under Alabama Medicaid's rule a provider of rehabilitative mental health services must be certified by the Department as a 310-board community mental health center, which in these two counties means WellStone [367]. Care coordination for Medicaid recipients is organized by region; Madison and Cullman share the Northeast region with Jackson, Limestone, Marshall and Morgan [368]. <!-- b: B0246 -->

Coverage of stays in psychiatric institutions has a hole for working-age adults; how Medicaid pays for stays in general-hospital psychiatric units was not read for this report. Medicaid's psychiatric facility rule covers inpatient psychiatric care for recipients under 21 [369]. For those aged 21 to 64, Alabama's federal waiver pays for stays in institutions for mental disease only in Baldwin and Mobile counties, and no north Alabama facility is covered [370]. Statewide, Medicaid spent $703 million on mental health in FY2025, 7% of its total; no breakdown of that spending by county was found in the agency's documents as read [58] [358]. <!-- b: B0247 -->

<!-- section: s12-the-ccbhc-transition -->
### The CCBHC transition

WellStone was one of the first two clinics in Alabama to enter the federal demonstration, certified by the state effective July 2024 [119] [42]. It had been preparing since 2022 on a federal implementation grant of $3,974,261, whose abstract set targets of 1,700 economically disadvantaged adults served in Madison County, 1,200 mobile crisis responses and 500 primary care screenings by September 2026 [39]. By June 2024 it said it had added primary care and an adult mobile crisis team [119]. <!-- b: B0248 -->

The payment works like this. For each day on which a Medicaid enrollee receives at least one qualifying service, the clinic is paid a single daily rate that covers everything delivered that day; each clinic's rate is rebuilt from its actual costs after its second demonstration year [371] [372]. WellStone's rate is not published. <!-- b: B0249 -->

The model's central tension is written into the state's own manuals. A clinic must provide the full range of services to anyone who seeks care, regardless of ability to pay or place of residence [373] [374]. The provider manual says the daily rate "will not be paid for services provided to populations outside of those enrolled in Medicaid" [57]. In Madison County, where fewer than one resident in five was eligible for Medicaid at some point in FY2024 [58], and in a state without expansion, that leaves every adult who is not enrolled in Medicaid outside the new payment; how many of WellStone's clients that is could not be established from the documents read. WellStone states that no one is turned away for inability to pay, reports nearly $3.4 million in uncompensated care for FY2025, and names medication for adults with no payer as an unmet need [48] [1]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0250 -->

What has the model produced? The Department's answer to legislators was two access measures: days to first evaluation down from 18 to under one, and days to first therapy from 33 to eight, over 2025 [50]. Its annual report counts 13,700 people served by WellStone as a clinic in FY2025 [64]. No other measured result was found. The demonstration requires 15 to 17 outcome measures, and no results for its first year, which ended in mid-2025, were found in any document read [42]. <!-- b: B0251 -->

The model is spreading around WellStone. Mountain Lakes, which serves Jackson and Marshall counties in the ring, received federal approval in October 2025, and the Department's board was told that five more centers would convert in 2026 [46] [115]. The centers for Morgan, Lawrence and Limestone and for the Shoals were not among the five clinics in the Department's directory as read for the statewide report, and are not among the six centers a September 2026 press report listed as receiving grants to pursue certification; one ring center, Northwest Alabama Mental Health Center, is on that list [375]. <!-- b: B0252 -->

<!-- section: s12-commercial-insurance-and-hospitals -->
### Commercial insurance and the hospital market

The commercial market has one dominant insurer and, since 2026, one dominant hospital system. Blue Cross and Blue Shield of Alabama held 82% of the commercial health insurance market in the Huntsville metropolitan area at the start of 2024, by the American Medical Association's measure [376]. On 31 March 2026 the Huntsville Hospital Health System completed its purchase of Crestwood Medical Center from a for-profit chain, which put Huntsville Hospital and Crestwood in one system [49]. A news report at the time recorded concerns about competition and cost and no action by a regulator [377]. <!-- b: B0253 -->

The system's finances bear on mental health mainly through what it absorbs. Its audit puts uncompensated care at about $144.4 million for the year ended June 2025, down from $171.4 million the year before, a fall it attributes mainly to higher Medicaid access payments [125]. In December 2025 the system expected the figure to rise by $10 million to $15 million in 2026 if marketplace subsidies expired; no later figure was found [364]. The audit, as read, contains no figure for behavioral health revenue, cost or volume [125]. The system's chief executive said in April 2026 that over half its reimbursement comes from Medicare and Medicaid at below-cost rates [49]. <!-- b: B0254 -->

## Section 13. The Ring: Thirteen Counties, Four Centers

<!-- section: s13-scope -->
### Scope

This section has no counterpart in the statewide report. It describes, in outline only, the thirteen counties beyond Madison and Cullman whose crises the state routes to Huntsville, and the four community mental health centers that serve them for everything else. It does not examine their courts, schools, hospitals or housing. Its purpose is to show what the crisis center's wider catchment looks like and how much of that catchment's use of the center is known. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0261 -->

<!-- section: s13-four-centers -->
### Four centers

The Department assigns the thirteen ring counties to four centers: Mountain Lakes Behavioral Healthcare for Jackson and Marshall; the Mental Health Center of North Central Alabama for Lawrence, Limestone and Morgan; CED Mental Health Center for Cherokee, DeKalb and Etowah; and Northwest Alabama Mental Health Center for Fayette, Lamar, Marion, Walker and Winston [2]. Its FY2025 report counts 3,129 people served by Mountain Lakes, 2,694 by North Central, 2,558 by CED and 3,306 by Northwest, against WellStone's 11,659 [64]. The centers' own figures run higher: Northwest's plan reports 10,317 consumers in 2023, a count that includes substance use and disability services, and no source reconciles the two [378]. <!-- b: B0262 -->

**Mountain Lakes** is the only other Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic in the fifteen counties, approved in October 2025 [46]. It says it runs its own mobile crisis team in both of its counties around the clock, pairing a clinician with a peer specialist or case manager; its crisis page says the team may arrange inpatient admission and does not mention the Huntsville center [379]. <!-- b: B0263 -->

**North Central Alabama**, based in Decatur, says it treated over 3,525 people in 2023 with about 140 staff across sites in Decatur, Athens and Moulton [380] [381]. It broke ground in December 2024 on a 16-bed crisis residential unit in Decatur for people under commitment, due for completion in October 2025 [382]. In October 2026 it was advertising for nurses for the unit, and no source confirmed that it had opened [383] [384]. Its chief executive said at the groundbreaking that crisis residential facilities in the region had existed only in Huntsville, Cullman, Jasper and Anniston for a decade [382]. <!-- b: B0264 -->

**CED** has one office in each of its three northeastern counties. Its published crisis service is a 24-hour telephone line; the parts of its plan and website that were read name no mobile crisis team or crisis residential unit of its own, and its plan lists transportation as a continuing need [385] [386] [387]. <!-- b: B0265 -->

**Northwest** covers five counties: Fayette, Lamar, Marion, Walker and Winston. It runs mobile crisis in two of them, Marion and Winston, a 16-bed crisis residential unit for adults at Townley in Walker County that takes referrals statewide, and by contract a 24-bed behavioral unit at the Jasper hospital [378]. <!-- b: B0266 -->

<!-- section: s13-what-the-ring-looks-like -->
### What the ring looks like

The ring is more rural, poorer and thinner in providers than the core. Six of the thirteen counties are classed as entirely rural [20]. Median household income runs from $49,488 in Fayette to $83,629 in Limestone, and the uninsured share of people under 65 from 8.4% in Limestone to 14.6% in DeKalb [6]. Residents per mental health provider range from 339 in Marshall to 4,774 in Winston [20]. Walker County's drug overdose death rate, 69.1 per 100,000, is the highest of the thirteen counties with a published rate and nearly twice Cullman's [20]. Every ring county carries a federal mental health shortage designation [52]. Appendix A gives the figures county by county. <!-- b: B0267 -->

The ring holds inpatient psychiatric beds. Decatur Morgan West has 64 beds, Mountain View Hospital in Gadsden 68, and hospitals in Boaz, Guntersville, Gadsden and Jasper list psychiatric services without bed counts [388] [389] [390] [391] [392] [393]. Several ring hospitals belong to or are affiliated with the Huntsville Hospital system, among them Athens-Limestone Hospital, Decatur Morgan Hospital, the Marshall Medical Centers, Highlands Medical Center and DeKalb Regional Medical Center [394]. <!-- b: B0268 -->

<!-- section: s13-how-much-the-ring-uses-huntsville -->
### How much the ring uses Huntsville

No source read for this report says where the crisis center's patients come from. The Department's report gives 1,879 served at the adult unit and 520 at the pediatric unit with no county of residence, and WellStone's plan gives a monthly total with none [64] [1]. The Department's crisis pages say nothing about distance or transport from the thirteen counties; the only ways in they describe are walking in and being brought by a first responder [7]. <!-- b: B0269 -->

The state's evaluators offered the one piece of evidence there is, and it is indirect. Crisis centers, they reported in 2025, may serve fewer people from communities outside the metropolitan areas where they sit, and peer staff said they wished they saw more people from farther away [62]. They also recorded that people face transport barriers when they leave [62]. Taken with the ring centers' own mobile teams and residential units, this suggests that the ring's dependence on Huntsville is smaller than the catchment map implies, and that for the counties nearest the Birmingham and Tuscaloosa catchments the assignment may exist mostly on paper [3]. No number in the sources read for this report can confirm or refute that. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0270 -->

<!-- section: s13-overlapping-boundaries -->
### Overlapping boundaries

The fifteen counties share a crisis center and little else. They are divided among six area agencies on aging, four homelessness continuums and three Medicaid care coordination regions [395] [311] [396]. The Department's Region I Community Services office is in Decatur and lists thirteen counties, not the same thirteen; its page does not say which division's regions these are [10].
